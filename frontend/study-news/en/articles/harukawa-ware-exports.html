Springvale ware exports hit a record ¥2.84 billion as overseas buyers seek out small kilns

Exports of the city's pottery rose 22% last year and have more than tripled since 2016, led by the United States and France. But potters warn that the boom is stretching workshops with too few hands.

Y
By Yui HamadaBusiness and transport reporter
Published Mon 28 Sep 2026, 06:00 JST
Share this article
2 min read

Exports of Springvale ware reached a record ¥2.84 billion in 2025, up 22% from ¥2.33 billion the year before, according to figures released by the Springvale Ware Cooperative. It was the fifth consecutive year of growth and more than three times the ¥0.92 billion exported in 2016.

The cooperative's 63 member kilns, which employ 410 people, produced pottery worth ¥7.9 billion last year, meaning that more than a third of everything made in Springvale's kilns now leaves the country.

The United States was the largest market, taking 31% of exports by value, followed by France with 18%, Taiwan with 14% and Australia with 9%. Other countries accounted for the remaining 28%. Exports fell to ¥0.97 billion in 2020, when restaurants and shops abroad closed during the pandemic, but have grown every year since.

From tea bowls to dinner tables

The growth has come less from the expensive tea bowls for which Springvale is known than from everyday tableware: rice bowls, small plates and cups in the blue-grey and amber ash glazes that buyers abroad now associate with the city. Restaurants in New York, Paris and Taipei that serve Japanese food have become regular customers, and several kilns sell directly to overseas shoppers through their own websites.

Ten years ago a buyer from Paris would come once and order a few pieces for a gallery. Now they order two thousand rice bowls for a restaurant group and ask when the next batch is ready.

Hideo Maruyama, chair of the Springvale Ware Cooperative

A weak yen has made Springvale pottery cheaper abroad, and the exhibition of four centuries of Springvale ware at the city's museum of art has drawn foreign visitors and buyers this autumn.

Too few hands

The success has exposed a problem the industry has talked about for years. Of the cooperative's 410 workers, almost half are over 60, and many kilns are run by a single family. Some have stopped taking new orders until next spring.
