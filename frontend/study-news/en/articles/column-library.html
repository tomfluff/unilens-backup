What a library is for

The new Central Library will be a magnificent building. But for 52 years the old library at Shiroyama has offered something a building alone cannot: a public place you can walk to.

T
By Tomoko HayakawaGuest writer, retired school librarian
Published Sun 27 Sep 2026, 06:00 JST
Share this article
3 min read

I first walked into the library at Shiroyama in the spring of 1974, a few weeks after it opened. I was eleven, and I remember the smell of new shelves and the librarian who let me take out four books when the limit was three. Thirty-four years as a school librarian in this city began, I think, at that counter.

Next month the city opens its new Central Library beside Springvale Central Station, and the old building closes its doors for the last time on 18 October. I have been inside the new one on a tour for former staff, and I want to say plainly: it is wonderful. Five floors and 9,800 square metres. Room for 620,000 books. A whole floor for children, study rooms on the fourth floor, a café, and a rooftop terrace from which, on a clear day, you can see Mount Moonview. Most of all, 640 seats. Anyone who tried to find one of the old library's 180 seats in January, when every secondary school pupil in the city is revising, will know what that means.

A fair price

The building cost ¥6.2 billion, and it will cost about ¥1.31 billion a year to run, compared with ¥820 million for the old library. Some readers have written to this newspaper to say that is too much. I disagree. A library is the only indoor public place in this city where nobody asks you to buy anything, prove anything or leave by a certain time. It serves the child with nowhere quiet to do homework, the pensioner reading the newspapers, the job-seeker printing a CV, the visitor waiting for a train. Per resident, ¥1.31 billion is little more than ¥3,000 a year. I cannot think of a better bargain in the city budget.

What Shiroyama loses

My worry is not the new library. It is the neighbourhood it leaves behind. Shiroyama is an old district on a hill. Many of the people who use its library are over seventy, and for them the station is not a short walk but a bus ride each way. The six branch libraries will stay open, but none of them is in Shiroyama. The city says it will decide what to do with the old building by March next year.

I would ask the city to consider something modest. Keep one room of the old building open as a small reading room and lending point, with a few thousand books, the daily newspapers, and a librarian two or three days a week. Let readers order books from the Central Library and collect them there. It would cost a small fraction of the new building's running costs, and it would mean that the opening of one library does not mean the closing of another.

Remember the schools

One more request. The new library will have a children's floor, and that is good. But most children meet books first in school libraries, and in this city many of those are open only when a teacher can spare the time. Of the 59 elementary and junior high schools in Springvale, fewer than half have a school librarian for more than two days a week. If the city can find ¥6.2 billion for a building, it can find a little more for the people who teach children to use one.

I will be at the opening in November, I expect with a great many others. I will also be at Shiroyama on 18 October, to say goodbye to the counter where I took out four books instead of three. Both buildings, in their way, are what a library is for.
