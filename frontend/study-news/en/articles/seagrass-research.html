Harvest Bay's eelgrass meadows grow back by 38% in ten years, university study finds

Springvale University researchers mapped 569 hectares of eelgrass in the bay in 2025, up from 412 hectares in 2015 and the largest area in more than 30 years. Cleaner water and 1.9 million shoots planted by fishers made the difference, they say, but hot summers could undo the gains.

D
By Daisuke MizunoScience and health reporter
Published Wed 23 Sep 2026, 10:00 JST
Share this article
4 min read

The underwater meadows of Harvest Bay, which shrank to less than a third of their size in the second half of the 20th century, are recovering faster than expected, according to a study by Springvale University published this month in a peer-reviewed international journal of coastal ecology.

A team led by Professor Emi Kanazawa of the university's Graduate School of Environmental Science found that eelgrass, known locally as amamo, covered 569 hectares of the bay in 2025. That is 38% more than the 412 hectares recorded in 2015 and the largest area since the early 1990s, although it is still less than half of the 1,240 hectares surveyed in 1978.

From 1,240 hectares to 380

Eelgrass is a flowering plant that grows on sandy and muddy floors in shallow, clear water, usually less than five metres deep. Its meadows shelter young fish, squid and shellfish, trap sediment and store carbon in the sea floor. Older fishers in Springvale still call the shallows off Tidewater "the amamo field".

The meadows declined steadily from the late 1970s as land was reclaimed for Ferry Port and industrial sites, and as nutrients from household waste water fed algae that clouded the water. By 2005 only 380 hectares were left. At the time, water transparency, measured by lowering a white disc into the sea until it can no longer be seen, averaged 3.1 metres at the bay's monitoring stations.

When I started diving here as a student, you could swim for ten minutes over bare mud. Now, in places, you have to push your way through the leaves.

Professor Emi Kanazawa, Springvale University

Cleaner water and 1.9 million shoots

The study attributes the recovery to two changes. In 2012 the Harvest Bay treatment plant, which handles waste water for most of Springvale, introduced advanced treatment that removes much of the nitrogen and phosphorus. Transparency has since improved to an average of 4.6 metres in 2025, letting enough light reach beds that had been too dark for eelgrass to grow.

The second change came from the water itself. Since 2011, members of the Harvest Bay Fishers' Cooperative have planted 1.9 million eelgrass shoots, raised from seed in tanks at the cooperative's hall and set by hand from small boats each spring and autumn. The researchers compared planted sites with bare sites of the same depth and sediment nearby, and found that planted sites were far more likely to grow into continuous meadows.

We did it for the fish, not for a research paper. But it is good to see in numbers what we have been seeing from the boats for years.

Isamu Takeda, head of the Harvest Bay Fishers' Cooperative

To map the meadows, the team combined drone and satellite images with dives at 120 survey points around the bay, where divers counted shoots and checked what the images showed. Figures for earlier years came from aerial photographs and prefectural surveys, which the team re-analysed with the same method so that the decades could be compared.

The study in numbers

  • Eelgrass area: 1,240 ha (1978), 380 ha (2005), 412 ha (2015), 569 ha (2025)
  • Change since 2015: +157 ha, or +38%
  • Water transparency: 3.1 m (2005), 4.6 m (2025)
  • Shoots planted by the fishers' cooperative since 2011: 1.9 million
  • Survey: 120 dive points, plus drone and satellite images
  • Carbon dioxide absorbed: about 1,700 tonnes a year

A carbon store that could earn money

Eelgrass meadows lock carbon into the sediment beneath them, so-called blue carbon. The researchers estimate that Harvest Bay's meadows now absorb about 1,700 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year.

Springvale City's environment division said it is studying whether the meadows could earn blue carbon credits, which companies buy to offset their own emissions, and that any income would go back into the replanting programme. The fishers' cooperative currently spends about ¥4 million a year on seed, tanks and fuel, half of it covered by a prefectural grant.

The returning meadows are already showing up in catches, Mr Takeda said. The cooperative's landings of young sea bream and of the small squid sold in Springvale's markets each spring have risen over the past five years, although the study did not measure fish numbers and Professor Kanazawa cautioned against drawing a direct line.

Hot summers are the next threat

The gains are fragile. In the summer of 2024, the temperature of the water in the shallows reached 30.8 °C, and the survey found that eelgrass had died back across parts of the bay shallower than two metres. Most of those beds recovered in 2025, but the team warns that repeated hot summers, and storms that tear plants from the sea floor, could reverse the trend.

Eelgrass does not tolerate water above 28 degrees for long. If summers like 2024 become normal, the shallowest meadows will either move deeper or disappear.

Professor Kanazawa

The team plans to repeat the full survey every five years and to place temperature loggers at 20 of the 120 points to monitor them each summer. The cooperative intends to shift its planting towards slightly deeper water, between two and four metres, from next spring.

Professor Kanazawa will present the findings in a free public lecture at Springvale University's Kita Campus on Saturday 24 October at 14:00. No booking is required.
