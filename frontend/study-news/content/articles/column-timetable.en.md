My mother is 84 and lives in the upper Kagami valley. She does not own a timetable. She has never needed one: she knows the last train home, and she knows that if she misses it, she sleeps at my house.

From 14 November, under the [timetable revision HaruTetsu announced on Tuesday](railway-timetable), several of those small certainties shift. Every Tsukikage limited express will stop at Tsukimi-guchi, a station where about 420 people board a day, and the run from Harukawa Central to Tsukimi Onsen will take 41 minutes instead of 39. The weekday last train at 23:20 will go on past Kamano to Kagami-kyō. Rapids will stop at Kawabata. At weekends the last train down from Tsukimi Onsen will leave at 21:40 instead of 21:05, and the Bayside Line will run every 15 minutes in the middle of weekdays.

Two minutes is the price of the Tsukimi-guchi stop, and some in the onsen town have already complained that their fast train is becoming slower. I think the two minutes are the best thing in the revision. A limited express exists to sell speed to visitors; when it stops for a village of a few thousand people, the railway is remembering that it also serves the people who live along its line.

That is what a timetable really is. Not a schedule but a promise about how a place works: this is when you can leave, this is when you can come home, this is how late you can stay out and still sleep in your own bed. Every line of the November timetable is a promise to someone: to the innkeepers who want their guests to finish dinner, to the night-shift workers of Kagami-kyō, to the families in the new flats by the port.

So it is worth asking who received no new promise.
