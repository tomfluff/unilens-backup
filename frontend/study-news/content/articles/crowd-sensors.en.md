When the floating lanterns were released from below Nishiki Bridge at last year's Harukawa Lantern Festival, the bridge above filled faster than stewards could clear it. For about 25 minutes nobody could move in either direction, and 14 people were treated for minor injuries.

This year the bridge will be watched by machines as well as people. Harukawa University and the city will trial 46 laser sensors, fixed to lamp posts on Riverside Avenue, Nishiki Bridge and Komachi-dori, that measure how densely people are packed and alert stewards before a crowd becomes dangerous. The trial covers both festival days, Saturday 10 and Sunday 11 October.

## How the sensors work

The sensors use lidar: they send out pulses of infrared laser light and time the reflections to build a three-dimensional cloud of points, refreshed ten times a second. Software developed in the laboratory of Professor Kenta Yoshimura, an information engineer at Harukawa University, picks out the clusters of points that are people and counts them in each square metre of pavement.

Unlike cameras, the sensors record no images, and the points cannot show a face, clothing or skin colour, Professor Yoshimura said. The counts are kept for 30 days for analysis and then deleted.

> A lidar sensor sees a person as about two hundred dots. It can tell you there are three people in a square metre. It cannot tell you who they are. -- Professor Kenta Yoshimura, Harukawa University

Twelve sensors will be on Nishiki Bridge and its approaches, 24 along the 1.3 km of Riverside Avenue between Nishiki Bridge and Asahi Bridge, and 10 on Komachi-dori. Installation begins on Tuesday 6 October, once Typhoon No. 21 has passed, and the system will be tested on the evening of Thursday 8 October.

## Two thresholds

The system works with two thresholds. When any section reaches two people per square metre, the stewards responsible for it receive an alert on their radios and phones and start directing people to other routes. At four people per square metre, entry to that section is stopped until the density falls. Crowd-safety researchers generally regard more than five people per square metre as dangerous, because people can no longer move freely and pressure builds through the crowd.

The busiest hours are expected between 19:30 and 21:00 on both evenings, when lanterns are floated from the Nishiki Bridge riverside steps beside the main stage and the lantern procession from Harukawa Castle reaches the river. Harukawa Railway may restrict entry to Shiromachi Station, four minutes from the main stage, from 18:00 to 21:30, and the control room will pass the readings for the bridge to station staff.

The festival's 620 stewards and 180 police officers will be coordinated from a control room at City Hall, where the readings appear on a map of the festival area.

> Last year we had stewards shouting into radios that the bridge was full. By then it was already too late. We need to know five minutes earlier. -- Hideki Saito, head of security, Lantern Festival Executive Committee

## A live map for visitors

Visitors will see a simplified version. From 17:00 on both days, the festival website will show a live congestion map of the riverside, the bridge and Komachi-dori in three colours: green for under one person per square metre, yellow for one to two, and red above two. The map is updated every 30 seconds.

## Cost and privacy

The trial costs ¥38 million and is paid for from the ¥0.3 billion set aside for festival safety in the city's [September supplementary budget](city-budget), which the council approved on 30 September. The same item pays for extra stewards and first-aid posts.

The city's personal information review board approved the trial on 25 September, on condition that no camera is added to the sensors and that signs explain the system at every entrance to the festival area.

Not everyone is convinced. "Today it is dots, tomorrow it could be cameras," said a member of a Shimo-Kawabata residents' group who attended a briefing for residents on Wednesday.

Professor Yoshimura's team will publish its results by March 2027. If the trial works, the city says, the sensors could also be used at Harukawa Central Station during large events and along the course of the Harukawa Castle Marathon in February.
