**Rating: 4/5**

It is hard to walk far in Springvale without meeting Springvale ware. It is on the counters of the noodle shops near the station, in the windows of Komachi-dori, in the gift catalogues that bring the city its furusato nozei donations. Familiarity can make a thing invisible. "Fire and Earth: Four Centuries of Springvale Ware", which opened at the Springvale City Museum of Art on 19 September and runs until 29 November, sets out to make us look again, and for the most part it succeeds.

With 214 works borrowed from 31 lenders, including temples, private collectors, two national museums and the 63 kilns working in the city today, it is the largest exhibition the museum has mounted since it opened. It moves chronologically through six rooms, from the first kilns established under the castle lords in the early 17th century to work made this year.

## A jar worth the ticket

The first two rooms are the strongest. The curators have resisted the urge to fill them, and the early pieces, mostly storage jars and plain bowls in the iron-rich local clay, have space to breathe. The thick, uneven ash glazes, running from grey-blue to amber where the flames hit them, look modern in a way that the later, more polished wares do not.

At the centre of the second room, alone in a free-standing case, is the so-called Moonview jar, made in the 1680s and lent by a temple in the hills below Mount Moonview. It is about 50 centimetres tall, slightly lopsided, with a pale glaze that has gathered into a glassy green pool on one shoulder. Legend says the potter left it in the kiln a day too long. Whatever happened, the result is the finest single object I have seen in a Springvale museum, and it alone is worth the ¥1,200 ticket.

> The jar has not been shown in public for 26 years. The temple agreed to lend it on condition that it travels nowhere else. -- Chief curator Shigeru Okabe

The tea bowls of the third room are quieter but rewarding: look for the small group made for the castle lords' tea gatherings, with their thumb-shaped dents and a pale crackle glaze.

## Too much of the Meiji boom

The exhibition stumbles in room four, devoted to the export wares made after Japan opened its ports. In the 1880s and 1890s, Springvale workshops produced huge vases, dinner services and decorative plates for buyers in Europe and America, painted with birds, flowers and the occasional startled tiger. The story is important: it was the first time Springvale's potters made their living from abroad, as they increasingly do again today.

But the curators have crammed more than 60 pieces into a room smaller than the first, and the effect is of a very good antique shop. Labels are small, set low and in grey type that is hard to read in the dim light. English labels, generous in the first two rooms, become a single short panel here. Most of all, I missed the people: who painted these plates, how they were paid, and what happened to the workshops when demand collapsed after the First World War.

## A moving final room

The last two rooms recover the momentum. Room five traces the 20th-century decline, when the number of kilns fell below 20, and the revival led by potters who returned to local clay and wood firing from the 1960s.

Room six is given over to contemporary work, including pieces by potters still in their twenties who sell mainly to buyers abroad. A wall of 120 everyday cups, one from each of the city's working potters past and present, which visitors are told they may touch, is a simple idea that gets the whole exhibition right: Springvale ware was always meant to be used.

> We wanted people to leave holding a cup, not just looking at a jar behind glass. -- Shigeru Okabe

The catalogue (¥2,800) is excellent, with new photographs of every work and an essay on the clay pits of the Mirror valley. The audio guide (¥600) is narrated in Japanese and English and is worth it for the first three rooms.

## Verdict

"Fire and Earth" is not perfect. The export room needs half its objects and twice its explanation, and visitors with less than perfect eyesight will struggle with the labels throughout. But at its best, in the Edo rooms and in that final wall of cups, it does what a local exhibition should: it sends you back into the city's shops and kitchens seeing the familiar as if for the first time. Go on a weekday morning, start slowly, and save some energy for the end.
