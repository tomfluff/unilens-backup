The smell inside the former Riverside textile warehouse is of wheat-starch glue and damp paper. On long tables under the sawtooth roof, rows of bamboo frames wait for their skins, and along the back wall finished lanterns are stacked to the beams, white and blank, waiting for the evening of Saturday 10 October.

Since 21 August, 160 volunteers have been making the 12,000 lanterns that will line the Mirror River, Castle Park and Komachi-dori during the 68th Springvale Lantern Festival on 10 and 11 October. That is 1,000 more than last year, and with ten days to go the workshop is turning out about 300 a day.

"We are on schedule, just," said Fumiko Shimizu, 64, who has run the workshop for twelve years. "Every year the committee asks for more lanterns, every year I say we cannot do it, and then we do it."

## From bamboo to light in three days

Each lantern begins as four strips of bamboo cut in winter from groves in the foothills of Mount Moonview and dried for six months. Volunteers bend and tie the strips into a frame about 45 cm tall, then paste on sheets of Moonview washi, a thick hand-made paper from a family workshop in the hills above Moonview Spa that has supplied the festival since the 1970s. The pasted lanterns dry for a day before an LED candle and a battery pack are fitted to the base.

Real candles were dropped in 2019, after two lanterns on Nishiki Bridge caught fire in strong wind the year before. The LEDs flicker slowly, a setting the volunteers spent a whole winter choosing. "People said it would look cheap," Ms Shimizu said. "Nobody has noticed."

Materials and batteries put the cost of each lantern at ¥1,850, or about ¥22 million for all 12,000. About a third of the frames are reused from last year; the rest are new.

## Aged 9 to 87

The oldest volunteer, Shigeru Hatano, is 87 and has worked on every festival since 1971, first as a carpenter building the river stages and, for the past 20 years, as the workshop's fastest frame-tier. He ties about 60 frames a day. The youngest is nine, a pupil at Riverside Elementary School who comes with her grandmother on Saturdays and is in charge of testing the LED candles.

Most volunteers are retired, but the workshop has added evening sessions from 18:00 to 21:00 on weekdays for people with jobs, and 41 of this year's 160 come only in the evenings.

> I look for my grandson's lantern every year. Last year it was a train. This year he says it is a secret. -- Shigeru Hatano, 87, volunteer

## Painted by 2,400 children

Not every lantern is blank. In September, 2,400 lanterns went out to pupils at 38 elementary schools, who painted them on the theme "My river". The lanterns that came back, decorated with herons, yuzu, trains and at least one very large fish, will hang along the riverside between Nishiki Bridge and Asahi Bridge, each school on its own stretch marked by a numbered post so families can find them.

## 3,000 lanterns for the river

A separate team makes the floating lanterns for the lantern floating, which runs from 19:30 to 21:00 on both evenings from the Nishiki Bridge riverside steps, beside the main stage near Castle Town Station. The 3,000 floating lanterns, 1,500 for each evening, have paper sides and a wooden base that break down in water, and nets at Minato Bridge collect them before they reach Harvest Bay.

They are sold at ¥1,000 each, with a paper slip on which buyers write a wish or the name of someone they have lost, at four places in the city and online until 9 October or until they sell out. By Tuesday more than two-thirds had been sold, the executive committee said.

Haruo Tanabe, 71, who chairs the committee, said the workshop was the part of the festival he worried about least. "The weather, the crowds, the trains: those keep me awake," he said. "The lanterns are always ready."
