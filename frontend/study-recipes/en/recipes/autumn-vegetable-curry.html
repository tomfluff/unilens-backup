Autumn Vegetable Curry (Japanese Curry from Scratch)

Thick, glossy Japanese-style curry made from scratch with a homemade roux, no boxed curry blocks, and full of autumn vegetables: kabocha, sweet potato, eggplant, lotus root and shimeji. Vegan as written.

Hana's best friend Rina has been coming over for dinner since they were both in kindergarten, and her family is vegetarian. The first time she stayed, I proudly made curry, and only afterwards read the box of curry roux: beef fat and pork extract. I was mortified. Since then I make the roux from scratch with oil, flour and curry powder, and to be honest, all of us prefer it. It tastes brighter, and it's less salty than the boxed kind.

Japanese curry was also the most popular school lunch in my nine years as a school nutritionist. Our version at the elementary school used whatever the farms in the Mirror valley sent that week, and in October that meant kabocha, sweet potatoes and the first lotus root from the fields near Harvest Bay. This curry is built on those autumn vegetables.

Why you'll love it

  • Real Japanese curry flavour without boxed roux: vegan, and you control the salt and the heat.
  • Five autumn vegetables, each cooked so it keeps its own texture.
  • Even better the next day, and the sauce freezes well.

Choosing the vegetables

Pick firm, glossy eggplants and a heavy kabocha. For lotus root, look for plump sections with pale, unbruised skin and closed ends; open ends let dirt inside. Cut everything to the sizes in the steps (my knife cuts guide shows half-moons and chunks). The order of cooking matters more than the cutting: lotus root and sweet potato go in first, kabocha later so it doesn't collapse, and the eggplant and shimeji are fried separately and added at the end.

Making the roux

A curry roux is just oil and flour cooked slowly until it's the colour of milky tea, then curry powder stirred in off the heat. Keep the heat low and keep stirring: it takes 8–10 minutes, and if it scorches, the whole pot tastes bitter. Add the curry powder only after you turn the heat off; the spices toast in the residual heat but burn on a direct flame. To add it to the curry, always mix a ladle of hot broth into the roux first, never the other way round, and you won't get lumps.

Tips for success

The flavour comes from the onions. Cook them for a full 15 minutes until they're deep golden and jammy, adding a spoonful of water if they catch. If you're in a hurry, microwave the sliced onions at 600 W for 5 minutes first and they caramelise in about half the time. Grated apple adds the gentle sweetness Japanese curry is known for, and garam masala at the very end brings back the fragrance that long simmering takes away.

For the stock I use a vegetable stock cube, or the kombu and shiitake dashi from my dashi guide. Serve it over plain rice (see the rice water ratios guide; 3 gō of rice feeds four curry-lovers), or with my autumn mushroom rice if you want to go all in on the season.

Equipment

  • Heavy-bottomed pot, about 22 cm, with a lid
  • Small frying pan (for the roux and the eggplant)
  • Wooden spatula
  • Whisk
  • Grater

Instructions

  1. Prepare the vegetables: soak the sweet potato in water for 5 minutes, and the lotus root in water with a few drops of vinegar for 5 minutes so it stays white, then drain both. Cut the kabocha into 3 cm chunks and separate the shimeji. Cut the eggplant just before frying, as it browns once cut.

  2. Heat 1 tbsp of the oil in the pot over medium heat. Add the onions with a pinch of salt and cook, stirring often, for 5 minutes until soft, then turn to medium-low and cook 10 minutes more, until deep golden brown, jammy and reduced to about a third. If they start to catch, add 1 tbsp water and scrape the bottom. Add the garlic and ginger for the last minute.

  3. Meanwhile, make the roux: put the 3 tbsp oil and the flour in a small frying pan over low heat and cook, stirring constantly, for 8–10 minutes, until it's the colour of milky tea and smells nutty. Turn off the heat, stir in the curry powder for 30 seconds, and scrape the roux into a bowl.

  4. Wipe out the frying pan. Cut the eggplant and toss it with the remaining 1 tbsp oil before it goes in the pan (so it doesn't soak it all up). Fry over medium-high heat, skin side down first, for 3 minutes until glossy and golden, add the shimeji and cook 2 minutes more, then set aside.

  5. Add the stock, grated apple, bay leaf, sweet potato and lotus root to the onion pot. Bring to the boil, skim off any foam, then cover and simmer over medium-low heat for 10 minutes.

  6. Add the kabocha, cover again and simmer for 8 minutes, until a skewer goes in easily but the pieces still hold their shape.

  7. Ladle about 200 ml of the hot broth into the bowl of roux and whisk until it's a smooth paste with no lumps, then pour it back into the pot, stirring. Add the eggplant and shimeji, soy sauce and ketchup. Simmer uncovered over low heat for 10 minutes, gently stirring the bottom every couple of minutes (it catches easily), until the curry coats the back of a spoon.

  8. Taste and add salt, up to ½ tsp. Stir in the garam masala, turn off the heat and remove the bay leaf. If it's too thick, add water 50 ml at a time; if too thin, simmer a few minutes more uncovered. Serve over rice, with fukujinzuke on the side.

Notes

  • The onions need a full 15 minutes; that's where the depth comes from. Short on time, microwave the sliced onions loosely covered at 600 W for 5 minutes before frying, and they'll take about 8 minutes in the pot.
  • If the roux smells burnt or has black specks, start again; burnt roux makes the whole pot bitter.
  • Japanese curry powder is medium-hot. For children, use 1½ tbsp and put chilli flakes on the table for the adults. My son Sota (9) eats it that way.
  • The curry thickens as it cools and even more overnight. Loosen it with a little water when you reheat it.
  • Food safety: don't leave a pot of curry out overnight. Thick stews cool slowly, and spore-forming bacteria (Clostridium perfringens) multiply in them. Divide it into shallow containers, refrigerate within 2 hours, and reheat until bubbling, stirring to the bottom.
  • The nutrition panel is for the curry only, without rice.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Lotus rootBurdock root (gobō), or carrot in thick slices
Sweet potatoPotato (May Queen holds its shape); or leave it out if you plan to freeze the curry
Grated apple1 tbsp mango chutney, or 1 tbsp honey (then it's no longer vegan)
All-purpose flourRice flour (komeko) for a gluten-free roux; also use gluten-free tamari instead of soy sauce
Vegetable stockKombu and dried shiitake dashi, or water with a little more salt at the end

Make ahead

Curry is better the next day: make it completely, cool it quickly and refrigerate it. You can also make the roux (with the curry powder) up to a week ahead and keep it in a jar in the fridge; it will be stiff, so mix it with hot broth as in step 7.

Storage

Keeps 3 days in the fridge. To freeze, take out the sweet potato (it turns mushy and watery when frozen) and freeze the rest for up to a month. Thaw overnight in the fridge and reheat in a pan until bubbling, stirring often and adding a splash of water.
