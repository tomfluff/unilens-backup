Milk Bread with Tangzhong (Japanese Shokupan)

A tall, cloud-soft Japanese milk loaf made with a cooked flour paste (tangzhong) that keeps it moist for days. Exact weights, photos of the dough, and what to do when the dough is sticky.

On Sunday mornings when I was little, my grandmother Fumi would clear away the breakfast trays at her inn in Tsukimi Onsen, sit down with a cup of tea, and toast a thick slice of shokupan from the bakery by the station, with butter and a spoonful of her own yuzu marmalade. That bakery closed years ago, and I spent a long time trying to bake a loaf that soft at home. The answer, it turned out, was a few spoonfuls of cooked flour paste.

Our recipe tester Kenta Mori, who baked bread professionally for years before he started checking my recipes, helped me get this one right, and it's now the loaf Hana and Sota want for school-morning toast. This week I'm baking two: one for us and one for my parents, who come down from Tsukimi Onsen every year for the Lantern Festival weekend.

Why you'll love it

  • Soft, fluffy and lightly sweet, and it stays soft for three days.
  • Exact weights, plus photos of how the dough should look after kneading and after the first rise.
  • Made entirely by hand; no mixer needed.

What is tangzhong?

Tangzhong (in Japanese, yudane) is a little of the flour cooked with milk until it reaches 65°C (149°F). At that temperature the starch swells and holds far more water than raw flour can, and that water stays in the bread, so the crumb is moist and springy and goes stale more slowly. You don't need a thermometer: when the whisk leaves clear trails and the paste looks like thick custard, it's ready. Always let it cool to room temperature before mixing; hot paste can kill the yeast.

Choosing the flour

Use bread flour (kyōrikiko) with about 12% protein; it makes the strong, stretchy gluten that holds a tall loaf. Weigh it if you possibly can. In US cups, the 300 g in the dough is about 2⅓ cups (130 g per cup, spooned in and levelled), but scooped cups can be 20% heavier, which is enough to make a dry loaf. My measurement conversions guide has the details.

Tips for success

The dough will be sticky. Very sticky for the first five minutes. Please don't keep adding flour: a tablespoon at most, or the bread turns dense. If it's sticking everywhere, cover it and walk away for 10 minutes, then knead by slapping and folding with a scraper in your other hand. The butter goes in once the dough starts to hold together, and you knead until it passes the windowpane test (step 4).

For rising, the dough likes 28–30°C (82–86°F); the proofing setting on an oven-microwave is perfect. Use the finger test rather than the clock. One thing that catches people out with combination ovens: you can't proof and preheat at the same time, so take the pan out a little early for the second rise and let it finish on the counter while the oven heats. My oven temperatures guide explains fan and non-fan settings.

And the hardest part: let the loaf cool for a full hour before you slice it. Warm bread squashes under the knife and the crumb turns gummy. If you want a sweet slice with it, my yuzu pound cake uses the same Kagami valley yuzu.

Equipment

  • 1.5-kin loaf pan (about 21 × 10 cm and 11 cm deep)
  • Digital kitchen scale
  • Small saucepan and whisk
  • Bench scraper (dough scraper)
  • Rolling pin
  • Cooling rack
  • Thermometer (optional, for the tangzhong and the dough)

Instructions

  1. Make the tangzhong: whisk the 20 g bread flour into the 100 ml milk in a small pan until there are no lumps. Cook over low heat, stirring constantly, for 2–3 minutes, until it thickens to a custard-like paste and the whisk leaves clear trails (65°C/149°F). Press cling film onto the surface and cool to room temperature, about 30 minutes. (Microwave option: 600 W for 30 seconds, stir, then 15–20 seconds more.)

  2. Put the 300 g bread flour, sugar and yeast in a large bowl, with the salt on the other side so it doesn't sit on the yeast. Add the cooled tangzhong, the beaten egg (minus the tablespoon for brushing) and the milk, and mix with a spatula for about 1 minute, until no dry flour remains.

  3. Turn the dough onto a clean counter and knead for 5 minutes, pushing it away with the heel of your hand and gathering it back with a scraper. It will be very sticky; that's normal, so add no more than 1 tbsp of flour. If it's unmanageable, cover it with the upturned bowl and rest 10 minutes, then slap and fold: lift the dough, slap it down onto the counter, fold it over itself, turn it 90° and repeat.

  4. Flatten the dough, put the softened butter in the middle, fold the dough over it and squeeze it in. The dough will turn slippery and fall apart; keep slapping and folding for 8–10 minutes until it is smooth, glossy and no longer sticks to the counter. Windowpane test: stretch a walnut-sized piece slowly between your fingers. It's ready when it forms a thin film you can see light through; if it tears with ragged edges, knead 2–3 minutes more and test again.

  5. Round the dough into a smooth ball by tucking the edges underneath, and put it seam side down in a lightly oiled bowl. Cover with cling film and leave at 28–30°C (82–86°F) for 50–60 minutes, until doubled in size. The proofing setting of an oven-microwave at 30°C is ideal.

  6. Finger test: dust a finger with flour and push it 2 cm into the centre of the dough. If the hole stays, it's ready. If it springs back and closes, wait another 10 minutes. If the dough sighs and collapses, it is over-proofed: carry on straight away and keep the second rise short.

  7. Press the dough gently to release the gas and divide it into 3 equal pieces by weight (about 210 g each). Round each into a ball, cover with a damp cloth or cling film and rest for 15 minutes. Meanwhile, grease the loaf pan with butter.

  8. Roll one piece into an oval about 12 × 18 cm. Fold the long sides in to the middle to make a strip about 8 cm wide, roll it out again to about 20 cm long, then roll it up tightly from the short end and pinch the seam closed. Repeat with the other two and set the rolls side by side in the pan, seams down.

  9. Cover and leave at 30–35°C for 40–50 minutes, until the tops of the rolls are 1 cm below the rim of the pan. If your oven does the proofing, take the pan out when the dough is about 2 cm below the rim and let it finish on the counter, covered, while the oven preheats to 180°C (350°F), or 160°C (320°F) for a fan oven.

  10. Brush the tops gently with the reserved egg, without letting it drip down the sides (it glues the loaf to the pan). Bake on the lower shelf for 28–30 minutes; after 15 minutes, once the tops are golden, lay a sheet of foil loosely over them. It's done when the top is deep golden brown, the sides are golden and the loaf sounds hollow when tapped, or 93°C (200°F) inside.

  11. As soon as it comes out, drop the pan onto the counter from about 10 cm to release the steam (this stops the sides caving in). Turn the loaf straight out onto a rack and cool for at least 1 hour before slicing.

Notes

  • Sticky dough is normal with tangzhong and gets smoother as the gluten develops. Lots of extra flour makes a dry, dense loaf; rest the dough for 10 minutes and use a scraper instead. Oiled hands also help.
  • Aim for a dough temperature of 26–28°C after kneading. In summer use milk straight from the fridge; in winter warm it to about 35°C.
  • If the sides cave in after baking (koshi-ore), the loaf was underbaked or the steam wasn't released. Bake 2–3 minutes longer, until the sides are golden, and drop the pan on the counter as soon as it comes out.
  • Stand mixer: knead 4 minutes on low, add the butter, then 6–8 minutes on medium. Bread machine: use the dough course, then shape and bake in the oven from step 7.
  • The nutrition panel is for the whole loaf. It cuts into 8 slices about 2 cm thick, each about 210 kcal.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Bread flourAll-purpose flour: use 10 ml less milk; the loaf is softer and rises a little less
Instant dry yeast5 g active dry yeast, dissolved in the lukewarm milk for 10 minutes first
MilkUnsweetened soy milk, or water plus 10 g skim milk powder
Unsalted butterSalted butter (cut the salt to 5 g), or 25 g vegetable oil added with the liquids
Egg50 ml milk, and brush the top with milk instead

Make ahead

Make the tangzhong the day before and keep it covered in the fridge; bring it back to room temperature before mixing. For fresh bread in the morning, do the first rise in the fridge overnight (8–12 hours) instead of at 30°C, then let the dough warm up for 30–60 minutes before dividing it.

Storage

Keep the loaf in a bag at room temperature for 2–3 days; the fridge makes bread go stale faster. To freeze, slice it, wrap each slice in cling film and freeze in a bag for up to a month. Toast straight from frozen.
