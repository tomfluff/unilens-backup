Kinoko Takikomi Gohan (Autumn Mushroom Rice)

Japanese rice cooked with shiitake, maitake, shimeji and aburaage in a vegan kombu-shiitake dashi. Rice cooker or clay pot, and the best onigiri of the year.

Every October, the road up to Tsukimi Onsen fills with cars on Sunday mornings. Half are heading for the hot springs; the other half, including us, are heading for the little farm stall outside Tsukimi Onsen station, where the Nishida family sells mushrooms they grow on logs in the cedar woods of Mount Tsukimi: fat shiitake, frilly maitake as big as a cabbage, and shimeji in tight little bunches. Hana chooses, Sota carries, and I try not to buy everything.

Kinoko takikomi gohan, rice cooked with mushrooms, is what I make that evening. My grandmother Fumi served it at her inn every autumn, cooked in a big clay pot, and the smell of soy sauce and shiitake drifting down the corridor told the guests dinner was ready. This year Hana is in charge of the onigiri for our Lantern Festival picnic on 10 October, and she has already decided they'll be made with this.

Why you'll love it

  • Deep, savoury flavour from rice, mushrooms and a few pantry seasonings.
  • It's vegan, thanks to a dashi made from kombu and dried shiitake.
  • The rice cooker does the work, and leftovers make perfect onigiri.

Choosing the mushrooms

Use at least two kinds, one for aroma and one for texture. Shiitake gives depth, maitake a wonderful perfume (it colours the rice a little, too), and shimeji stay pleasantly bouncy. You need about 300 g in total for 2 gō of rice. Don't wash mushrooms; they soak up water and the rice turns soggy. Just wipe off any dirt with damp paper towel.

A dashi without fish

Most takikomi gohan uses bonito dashi, but kombu and dried shiitake give all the savoury depth you need and keep it vegan. Soak them in water for at least 30 minutes, or overnight in the fridge if you're organised. Thin dried shiitake (kōshin) soften in 30 minutes; thick, cracked donko need the whole night. My dashi guide has more on both.

Rice cooker or donabe?

Both work, and the liquid is exactly the same: 330 ml of dashi plus the seasonings, about 390 ml in all for 2 gō of rice. In a rice cooker, add the seasonings first and then the dashi up to the 2-gō line. A donabe (clay pot) gives you okoge, the crisp golden crust on the bottom that everyone fights over. For other quantities, see my rice and water ratios.

Tips for success

Soak the rice in plain water before adding the seasonings, because salt and soy sauce slow down how fast rice absorbs water. Don't stir the mushrooms in before cooking: lay them on top so the water can circulate around the rice. And let it rest for 10 minutes before you open the lid, so the moisture evens out.

Equipment

  • Rice cooker (for at least 3 gō) or a donabe clay pot for 2–3 gō (a heavy pot with a tight lid also works)
  • Bowl or jug for soaking the kombu and shiitake
  • Fine sieve for draining the rice
  • Rice paddle (shamoji)
  • Measuring jug

Instructions

  1. Put the kombu and dried shiitake in a bowl or jug with the 400 ml of room-temperature water and leave them to soak for at least 30 minutes (or overnight in the fridge). If the shiitake float, weigh them down with a small plate.

  2. Meanwhile, wash the rice: cover it with water, swirl briefly and pour the water off straight away, because the rice absorbs the first water. Then rub it gently with your fingertips about 20 times, rinse, and repeat 3–4 times until the water is only slightly cloudy. Soak the rice in plenty of fresh water for 30 minutes, then drain it in a sieve.

  3. Gently squeeze the soaked shiitake over the bowl (the liquid is your dashi), cut off the stems and slice thinly. Slice the fresh shiitake 5 mm thick, cut the base off the shimeji and separate them into small clusters, and tear the maitake into bite-size pieces. Cut the carrot into 3 cm matchsticks (see my knife cuts guide). Pour boiling water over both sides of the aburaage to remove excess oil, pat dry, halve lengthwise and slice into 5 mm strips. Lift out the kombu and keep it, then measure 330 ml of the dashi, topping up with water if needed.

  4. Rice cooker: put the drained rice in the inner bowl and add the soy sauce, mirin, sake and salt. Then add dashi up to the 2-gō line for white rice (about 330 ml). Stir once, gently, just to mix the seasonings into the liquid, and level the surface.

  5. Spread the mushrooms, carrot and aburaage evenly over the rice and lay the kombu on top. Don't stir them in. Close the lid and cook on the regular white rice setting, or the takikomi setting if your cooker has one (not quick-cook).

  6. Donabe (instead of steps 4–5): put the drained rice in the clay pot with the 330 ml dashi and all the seasonings, level it, and top with the mushrooms, carrot, aburaage and kombu. Cover and cook over medium-high heat until it boils hard and steam puffs from the hole in the lid, about 10–12 minutes. Turn the heat to the lowest setting and cook for 13 minutes. For okoge, turn the heat to high for the last 10–15 seconds, until you hear crackling, then turn it off.

  7. Leave the rice to rest for 10 minutes without opening the lid: in the donabe after turning off the heat, in the rice cooker after it beeps.

  8. Remove the kombu (slice it into thin strips and mix it back in, if you like). With a damp rice paddle, cut through the rice in a cross, then fold it gently from the bottom up so the mushrooms are evenly mixed without squashing the rice.

  9. Serve topped with mitsuba or green onion and a sprinkle of sesame. For onigiri, wet your hands, rub them with a little salt and shape about 100 g of rice into each triangle.

Notes

  • Don't add extra liquid if you add more mushrooms; they release water as they cook. This liquid works for up to about 400 g of mushrooms.
  • Hard rice usually means it wasn't soaked before the seasonings went in, or the toppings were mixed into the rice. Mushy rice means too much liquid or stirring before cooking.
  • To make 3 gō, multiply everything by 1.5 and fill the dashi to the 3-gō line. The line, not the scaled number, is what counts in a rice cooker.
  • This liquid is for white rice. Brown rice and mixed grains need more water and a longer soak; see the rice and water ratios guide.
  • In a heavy pot instead of a donabe, use the same timing but check for scorching: pots heat faster, so 12 minutes on the lowest heat may be enough.

Make ahead

Soak the kombu and shiitake overnight in the fridge and prep the mushrooms in the morning (keep them in a bag in the fridge). Don't use the rice cooker's delay timer for this recipe: the rice would sit in soy sauce for hours, which leaves it hard and, in warm weather, isn't safe.

Storage

Don't leave it on keep-warm for more than 2–3 hours; it dries out and the soy smell gets strong. It keeps for 2 days in the fridge (reheat in the microwave with a sprinkle of water). To freeze, wrap 150 g portions flat in cling film while still warm, cool, and freeze for up to a month; microwave from frozen at 600 W for about 3 minutes. Onigiri freeze the same way.
