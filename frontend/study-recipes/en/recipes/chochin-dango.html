Chōchin Dango (Lantern Festival Dango with Mitarashi Glaze)

The Harukawa Lantern Festival snack: chewy rice dumplings, four to a skewer, toasted and coated in a glossy sweet-salty mitarashi glaze until they glow like little lanterns.

On the second weekend of October, the banks of the Kagami River turn into a river of light. Thousands of paper lanterns hang between the willows from Harukawa Castle down to the bay, and food stalls line both sides of the path. This year the Harukawa Lantern Festival falls on Saturday 10 and Sunday 11 October, and if you go, follow the smell of soy sauce caramelising over charcoal. That is chōchin dango, lantern dango: four chewy rice dumplings on a skewer, toasted and glazed with mitarashi sauce until they glow amber, exactly like the lanterns above them.

The most famous stall belongs to Kagamiya, a sweet shop by the castle moat that has been grilling them at the festival since 1958. When I was Hana's age, my grandmother Fumi took the train down from Tsukimi Onsen just to buy a box for the inn. My kids would happily queue for half an hour, but I have been making them at home for years, and shaping them has become a family job: Sota rolls the balls, Hana does the skewers, and Daisuke eats the ones that come out lopsided.

Why you'll love it

  • A handful of pantry ingredients and no oven.
  • Children love rolling the dough.
  • The glossy glaze is ready in three minutes.

Shiratamako, joshinko or tofu?

Shiratamako (glutinous rice flour) makes the dumplings soft and stretchy; joshinko (non-glutinous rice flour) makes them firmer, with a clean bite, and helps them keep their round shape on the skewer. Half and half gives you the texture of the festival stalls. Fumi made hers with shiratamako alone and silken tofu instead of water: tofu dango stay soft for longer, which helps if you are making them a few hours ahead. The details are in the substitutions.

Getting the dough right

Rice flours absorb water differently from bag to bag, so the water is a range. Aim for 'as soft as an earlobe' (mimitabu, the classic Japanese description): smooth, springy and not sticky. Cracks mean the dough is too dry; sticky fingers mean too wet. Both are easy to fix, see step 2.

Tips for success

Make the balls the same size so they cook evenly, and don't skip the ice bath: it is what gives them their shine. Dango harden as they cool because the starch firms up, so eat them on the day, ideally within a couple of hours. For small children and older family members, cut each dumpling into halves or quarters: like mochi, they are chewy and can be a choking risk. Having a festival night at home? Add daigaku-imo and karaage to the table.

Equipment

  • Large pot (at least 2 litres of water) and a slotted spoon
  • Large bowl of iced water
  • 8 bamboo skewers, about 15 cm
  • Small saucepan and a heatproof spatula
  • Non-stick frying pan, or a wire rack for toasting over a gas flame (optional)
  • Kitchen scale

Instructions

  1. Put the shiratamako and joshinko in a bowl and crush any lumps of shiratamako with your fingers. Add about three-quarters of the lukewarm water and mix with your hand, squeezing the dough through your fingers until it comes together.

  2. Keep kneading for 2–3 minutes, adding the rest of the water a tablespoon at a time, until the dough is as soft as an earlobe: smooth, springy and not sticking to your hand. If a ball cracks at the edges when you press it flat, it is too dry: add water 1 tsp at a time. If it sticks to your fingers or slumps, it is too wet: knead in shiratamako 1 tsp at a time.

  3. Roll the dough into a log and cut it into 32 equal pieces, about 11 g each (weigh the first one). Roll each piece between your palms into a smooth ball about 2.5 cm across and line them up on a tray. Cover with a damp cloth so they don't dry out.

  4. Bring a large pot of water (about 2 litres) to the boil and set the bowl of iced water next to it. Drop in half the dango and stir gently once so they don't stick to the bottom. When they float, after about 2 minutes, cook for 3 minutes more.

  5. Lift them straight into the iced water with the slotted spoon and leave for 1–2 minutes; this firms them up and makes them glossy. Cook the second batch the same way, then drain all the dango well on kitchen paper.

  6. Thread 4 dango onto each skewer, pushing them close together. For the festival look, toast them: heat a non-stick frying pan over medium heat (no oil) and cook for 1–2 minutes per side until browned in spots, or scorch them lightly on a wire rack over a gas flame (soak the skewers in water for 10 minutes first so they don't burn).

  7. For the sauce, put the water, sugar, soy sauce, mirin and potato starch in a small saucepan and stir until dissolved; stir again just before heating, as the starch settles. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly. When it bubbles and turns from cloudy to clear and glossy, keep stirring for 30 seconds more, then take it off the heat. It should coat the back of a spoon.

  8. Spoon the warm sauce over the skewers, or roll them through it in the pan, and serve straight away. Dango are softest within an hour or two; they firm up as they cool.

Notes

  • Yuzu variation: stir 1 tsp yuzu juice and ½ tsp finely grated yuzu zest into the sauce off the heat. This is Kagamiya's autumn special.
  • If the sauce stays thin, the starch hasn't cooked yet: put it back on the heat and stir until it bubbles and turns clear. If it is too thick or sets like jelly as it cools, loosen it with 1–2 tsp hot water.
  • Choking risk: cut dango into small pieces for small children and older people, and have them eat sitting down.
  • Rice flours are gluten-free, but ordinary soy sauce contains wheat. Use tamari for a gluten-free glaze.
  • Dango that have gone hard soften again in the microwave: 10–20 seconds per skewer at 600 W, loosely covered.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
JoshinkoAll shiratamako (softer, stretchier dango), or Thai white rice flour
ShiratamakoMochiko (glutinous rice flour); it absorbs water faster, so add the water gradually
Water in the dough (tofu version)200 g shiratamako (no joshinko) + about 200 g silken tofu and no water; add the tofu a little at a time. These stay soft for several hours.
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
Potato starchCornflour (cornstarch), same amount

Make ahead

Boiled, unsauced dango freeze well: freeze them spread out on a tray, then bag them for up to a month. Drop them frozen into boiling water for 1–2 minutes until soft, cool them in iced water and carry on from step 6. The sauce keeps for 3 days in the fridge; rewarm it with a splash of water.

Storage

Dango are best on the day. In the fridge they turn hard within hours, so keep them covered at cool room temperature and finish them the same day. Revive hard ones in the microwave (600 W, 10–20 seconds per skewer, loosely covered) or by toasting them again in a frying pan.
