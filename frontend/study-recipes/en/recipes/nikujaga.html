Nikujaga (Japanese Meat and Potato Stew)

My grandmother's sweet-salty beef and potato stew, simmered under a drop lid in dashi. One pot, 45 minutes, and even better the next day.

The first evening in October that I need a cardigan in the kitchen, I make nikujaga. It's practically a law in our house. This year it was last Tuesday: the cicadas have finally gone quiet, the yuzu at the greengrocer by Springvale Central have started turning from green to yellow, and Sota came home from soccer practice and asked, before he'd even taken his shoes off, “Is it potato stew weather yet?” It was.

Nikujaga (肉じゃが) simply means “meat and potatoes”, and every Japanese family makes it a little differently. Mine comes from my grandmother Fumi, who ran a small inn in Moonview Spa. She served it to hikers coming down from Mount Moonview in a big Springvale-ware bowl and left the pot on the stove for seconds. I've changed exactly one thing: I add the snow peas at the end instead of simmering them grey. Sorry, Grandma.

Why you'll love it

  • One pot, about 45 minutes from cutting board to table, and most of that is gentle simmering.
  • Sweet, salty and deeply comforting; Hana has eaten it since she had teeth.
  • It is genuinely better the next day, so it's perfect for making ahead.

Choosing the potatoes

This decision changes the dish most. May Queen (メークイン) potatoes are long and waxy and hold their shape. Danshaku (男爵) are round and floury: their edges crumble into the sauce and thicken it, which is how Fumi liked it. I use Danshaku at home and May Queen when I take nikujaga to a potluck. Outside Japan, Yukon Gold or Charlotte behave like May Queen, and Russets like Danshaku. Cut them large, about 4 cm, and soak them in water for 5 minutes so the surface starch doesn't cloud the sauce.

Beef or pork?

Western Japan uses beef and eastern Japan uses pork; Springvale sits in the middle and argues about it at every PTA dinner. I use thinly sliced beef chuck sold as sukiyaki cut, or cheaper offcuts (切り落とし). Thinly sliced pork belly or shoulder is just as good and a little richer.

Tips for success

Sugar goes in before the soy sauce. Sugar soaks in slowly, while salt tightens the surface, so if the soy sauce goes in first the potatoes never get properly sweet. Use a drop lid (otoshibuta), or a circle of baking paper with a small hole in the middle: it bastes everything with only 400 ml of liquid and stops the potatoes tumbling and breaking. Keep the shirataki away from the beef, because the calcium in konnyaku can toughen the meat. And once the liquid is in, don't stir; shake the pot instead.

The real secret is cooling. The flavour soaks into the potatoes as they cool, not while they bubble, so if you have time, turn off the heat, leave it for 30 minutes and warm it up just before dinner. Homemade dashi makes a difference, but instant granules are fine on a weeknight. If you love simmered dishes, try my mackerel simmered in miso next, with a bowl of autumn mushroom rice.

Nikujaga (Japanese Meat and Potato Stew)

Ingredients

For simmering

Quantities in the instructions are for the original recipe.

Equipment

  • Medium pot (about 22 cm) with a lid
  • Otoshibuta (drop lid) or a circle of baking paper with a small hole cut in the centre
  • Small saucepan for blanching the shirataki and snow peas
  • Bamboo skewer for testing the potatoes
  • Ladle or fine skimmer for the scum

Instructions

  1. Peel the potatoes, cut them into 4 cm pieces and soak them in a bowl of water for 5 minutes, then drain. Cut the onion into 8 wedges, the carrot into bite-size wedges, and the beef into 5 cm pieces.

  2. Bring a small saucepan of water to the boil. Blanch the snow peas for 30 seconds, lift them out and set aside. Cut the shirataki into 2 or 3 lengths with kitchen scissors, boil them in the same water for 2 minutes to remove the smell, and drain.

  3. Heat the oil in the pot over medium heat and fry the beef for about 1 minute, until most of the pink is gone. Add the potatoes, onion and carrot and stir for 2 minutes, until the edges of the potatoes look slightly translucent and everything is coated in oil.

  4. Add the shirataki on the opposite side of the pot from the beef, pour in the dashi and bring to the boil over high heat. Skim off the grey scum that rises.

  5. Add the sake, sugar and mirin, lay the drop lid directly on the food and simmer over medium-low heat for 5 minutes. The liquid should bubble gently up around the lid.

  6. Add the soy sauce, put the drop lid back and simmer for another 12–15 minutes, until a skewer slides easily into the potatoes and the liquid has reduced to about a third. Don't stir; give the pot a gentle shake now and then so nothing sticks.

  7. Turn off the heat, remove the drop lid, cover the pot with its own lid and leave it for at least 10 minutes (30 is even better) so the potatoes soak up the sauce as they cool.

  8. Warm through over medium-low heat if needed, then serve in bowls with some of the sauce and the snow peas on top.

Notes

  • The potatoes are done when a bamboo skewer slides into the thickest piece with no resistance and the liquid has reduced to about a third of the original depth.
  • New-crop potatoes in autumn are extra fluffy and cook faster; start checking 10 minutes after adding the soy sauce.
  • For a lighter taste, use 2½ tbsp soy sauce and add the last ½ tbsp only if it needs it after tasting at the end.
  • If you prefer more sauce for spooning over rice, add 100 ml extra dashi and leave the drop lid on until the end.
  • Snow peas go in at the very end so they stay bright green. Green beans (blanched 2 minutes) work too.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
BeefThinly sliced pork belly or shoulder, same amount
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar for each tbsp of mirin (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
SakeDry sherry or dry white wine, or water for no alcohol
DashiInstant dashi granules: about 1⅓ tsp dissolved in 400 ml hot water
ShiratakiLeave it out, or add a small handful of dried glass noodles (harusame) for the last 5 minutes

Make ahead

Make the whole stew the day before up to the end of step 7, cool it in the pot and refrigerate it. Reheat gently over medium-low heat with the lid on, and blanch the snow peas fresh just before serving.

Storage

Keeps for 3 days in an airtight container in the fridge. You can freeze it for up to a month, but take the potatoes and shirataki out first: frozen potatoes turn spongy and grainy, and shirataki goes rubbery. Add freshly boiled potatoes when you reheat it.
