Minato-nabe (Springvale Fisherman's Hot Pot with Sake Lees)

The harbour hot pot of Ferry Port: yellowtail, daikon and burdock in a creamy sake kasu and white miso broth, finished with udon or rice porridge.

Every Saturday from October to March, I set my alarm for 5:30, take an early Bayside Line train to Ferry Port and walk the two minutes from the station to the morning market while the sky over Harvest Bay is still pink. The boats come in around four, the auction is over by six, and when the market opens the fishmongers are already calling out prices over crates of squid, flounder and, in autumn, gleaming silver yellowtail. My first stop is always Okabe Fish, where Mr. Okabe keeps a bag of buri ara (the head, collars and bones) under the counter for me. It costs about 400 yen, and it is the secret to this pot.

Minato-nabe means 'harbour pot', and it was born on those piers. After the auction, the fishermen would light a fire in an oil drum, throw the fish nobody had bought into a pot with daikon and whatever vegetables were around, and stir in sake lees from the brewery up the road to keep warm in the wind off the bay. Today every Springvale family has its own version, and the pot comes out on the first chilly weekend of autumn, usually right after the Lantern Festival. Ours is a little sweeter than the dockside original because Daisuke and the kids like white miso, and Sota insists the best part is the udon at the end.

Why you'll love it

  • One pot on the table, and everyone helps themselves.
  • Sake lees make the broth creamy and deeply savoury without any dairy.
  • The leftover broth becomes a second meal: udon or rice porridge.

Choosing the fish

Bone-in pieces give the richest broth, but fillets work perfectly well and are easier to eat with children. Allow 500 g of fillets or about 700 g of ara for four. In October the yellowtail are still young and lean (the market calls the smaller ones warasa), so I like a mix: a couple of collars for flavour and two fillets for meat. Salmon, cod or Spanish mackerel are good substitutes. Whatever you use, don't skip salting and blanching. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the difference between a clean, sweet broth and a fishy one.

About sake kasu

Sake kasu (sake lees) is what is left after pressing sake. I buy mine from Shiomi Shuzō, a small brewery a few streets back from the port, which sells soft paste-style kasu (neri-kasu) all year and firm, freshly pressed sheets (ita-kasu) from December. Either works; sheets just need a warmer soak. Kasu still contains around 8% alcohol, and simmering removes most but not all of it, so for small children or during pregnancy, make the kasu-free version in the notes.

Tips for success

Use a good dashi (my dashi guide has the ratios), cut the vegetables so they cook evenly (thin sasagaki shavings for the burdock, see my knife cuts guide), and add the miso last, through a small strainer. Serve with a dab of homemade yuzu kosho, which cuts through the richness like nothing else. If you love fish and miso together, try my mackerel simmered in miso too.

Equipment

  • Donabe (clay pot), about 27 cm (size 9, for 3–4 people), or a heavy 24 cm pot
  • Tabletop gas burner (cassette stove), optional
  • Colander, a bowl of cold water and a kettle of boiling water for blanching the fish
  • Miso strainer or a small fine-mesh sieve
  • Small bowl and a whisk for the sake kasu

Instructions

  1. Cut the fish into 4–5 cm chunks (leave bone-in pieces as they are), sprinkle the salt over both sides and leave for 15 minutes. Beads of moisture will appear on the surface; that liquid is what smells fishy.

  2. Meanwhile, prepare the vegetables. Peel the daikon and cut it into 1 cm half-moons; cut the carrot into 7 mm rounds. Scrub the burdock with the back of a knife, shave it into thin sasagaki and soak in water for 5 minutes. Tear the konnyaku into bite-sized pieces with a spoon and boil for 2 minutes. Pour boiling water over the aburaage, squeeze it and cut into 2 cm strips. Slice the green onion diagonally 1 cm thick.

  3. Put the salted fish in a colander in the sink and pour boiling water over it until the surface turns white all over. Drop it straight into a bowl of cold water and gently rub off any scales, blood and slime, checking bone-in pieces carefully. Pat dry with kitchen paper.

  4. Tear the sake kasu into small pieces, put it in a bowl with 150 ml of the dashi (warm the dashi first if you are using firm sheets) and leave for 10 minutes. Mash and whisk until it is a smooth paste with no lumps.

  5. Pour the rest of the dashi into the donabe, add the daikon, carrot, drained burdock and konnyaku, and bring to the boil over medium heat. Lower the heat and simmer for 15 minutes, until the daikon looks translucent at the edges and a skewer slides in easily.

  6. Add the fish, aburaage, sake and mirin. Simmer gently for 5 minutes, skimming off the grey foam as it rises.

  7. Stir in the sake kasu paste and simmer on low heat for 5 minutes, scraping the bottom now and then so it doesn't catch. The broth turns milky white and smells sweet, a little like fresh bread.

  8. Put 3 tbsp of the miso in a miso strainer (or a ladle), lower it into the broth and dissolve it with chopsticks. Taste and add the last tablespoon only if needed. Add the soy sauce and green onion and simmer for 2–3 minutes without letting it boil hard.

  9. Bring the pot to the table on a tabletop burner and keep it at a gentle simmer. Serve in small bowls with yuzu kosho and shichimi on the side.

  10. For the shime, top the leftover broth up with about 200 ml water or dashi if it has reduced. For udon, add the noodles and simmer for 3 minutes. For porridge, rinse the rice briefly in a sieve, simmer it in the broth for 3–4 minutes, pour in the beaten eggs in a circle, cover, turn off the heat and wait 1 minute.

Notes

  • The fish is done when it is opaque all the way through and flakes at the thickest part: about 5 minutes for fillet chunks, 7–8 minutes for bone-in pieces.
  • Miso brands vary a lot in saltiness. Start with 3 tbsp, taste once the kasu has melted in, and add the fourth only if it needs it. The broth gets saltier as it reduces at the table, so top it up with a splash of dashi.
  • Alcohol: sake kasu contains about 8% alcohol and some always remains after simmering. For young children or during pregnancy, leave out the kasu and the sake, and stir in 100 ml unsweetened soy milk plus 1 extra tbsp white miso at step 8 instead. Heat it gently; soy milk separates if it boils hard.
  • Once the miso is in, keep the pot at a gentle simmer. A hard boil dulls the miso's aroma, and the kasu can catch on the bottom of a donabe.
  • Donabe care: never heat an empty donabe or one with a wet bottom, and let it cool completely before washing.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Yellowtail (buri)Salmon, cod (tara) or Spanish mackerel (sawara), same weight; cod needs only 4 minutes
Sake kasu100 ml unsweetened soy milk + 1 extra tbsp white miso (alcohol-free and milder)
SakeDry white wine or dry sherry, or water for no alcohol
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
Burdock (gobo)Leave it out, or add 4 fresh shiitake mushrooms with the fish

Make ahead

You can cook the broth and vegetables through step 5 the day before; cool, cover and refrigerate. Salt and blanch the fish on the day and carry on from step 6. The sake kasu paste (step 4) also keeps in the fridge for 3 days.

Storage

Leftovers keep for 2 days in the fridge; reheat gently until the fish is piping hot. Don't freeze the finished nabe: konnyaku turns spongy and daikon goes watery. Strained leftover broth freezes for a month and makes a wonderful miso soup base.
