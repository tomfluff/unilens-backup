Saba no Misoni (Mackerel Simmered in Miso)

★★★★★ 4.7 from 268 ratings by Natsuki Aoyama Updated September 18, 2026 7 comments

Fatty autumn mackerel simmered with ginger in a glossy, sweet-salty miso sauce. One pan, 30 minutes, and even better the next day.

Saturday mornings in autumn start early in our house. By 6:30 Daisuke and I are on the first Bayside Line train to Minori Port, and by 7 we're weaving between the crates at the morning market with a cool-box on wheels. In October the mackerel are at their best. Locals call it aki saba, autumn mackerel, and it's so fatty that the fishmongers' knives shine. My favourite stall, Uomasa, is run by Mrs. Takeda, who has been telling me which fish to buy for fifteen years and always throws in a knob of ginger.

Saba no misoni was one of the dishes my grandmother Fumi cooked most at her inn in Tsukimi Onsen. Her guests were mostly older couples who came for the hot springs, and she said a plate of this with rice and miso soup made them look like they'd come home. It's rich, glossy, sweet and salty, and the ginger keeps it fresh.

Why you'll love it

  • Ready in 30 minutes in a single pan.
  • Mackerel is inexpensive, full of omega-3s, and tastes even better the next day.
  • The sauce is so good you'll want a second bowl of rice.

Choosing the mackerel

Look for fillets with shiny, taut skin and firm, pinkish flesh, not dull or dry at the edges. If you're buying a whole fish, the eyes should be clear and the belly firm. Frozen and thawed Norwegian mackerel, sold in most supermarkets, is very fatty and works beautifully here. The one thing to avoid is salted mackerel (shio saba): it's already salty and will make the dish inedible.

The boiling-water trick

The step people skip, and shouldn't, is shimofuri: pouring boiling water over the fish until the surface turns white, then rinsing it in cold water. It takes two minutes and washes away the blood and slime that cause the fishy smell. Mrs. Takeda says it's the difference between a home cook and a good home cook.

Tips for success

Bring the simmering liquid to the boil before the fish goes in, so the surface sets straight away and the flesh doesn't fall apart or cloud the sauce. Use a drop lid so the fish is basted without being turned. And add the miso in two stages: half at the start to season the fish and mask any smell, and half at the end for aroma and that glossy coating, because miso loses its fragrance if it boils too long. Taste before adding the second half; brands vary a lot in saltiness.

Serve it with plain rice, miso soup and something green, or with mushroom rice for a proper autumn dinner. If you like this, my nikujaga uses the same drop-lid method.

Saba no Misoni (Mackerel Simmered in Miso)

Ingredients

For simmering

Quantities in the instructions are for the original recipe.

Equipment

  • Frying pan or shallow pan (20–24 cm), wide enough to hold the fish in one layer
  • Otoshibuta (drop lid) or a circle of baking paper with a small hole in the centre
  • Kettle for boiling water
  • Colander and a bowl of cold water
  • Small ladle and a spoon

Instructions

  1. If you have a half-side fillet, cut it in half crosswise. Score a shallow cross (or one diagonal slit) in the skin of each piece, so the skin doesn't curl and the sauce soaks in. Thinly slice half the ginger; cut the other half into very fine needles and soak them in cold water.

  2. Put the fish skin side up in a colander in the sink and pour boiling water from the kettle over it until the surface turns white, about 5 seconds per spot. Immediately transfer it to a bowl of cold water and gently rub off any blood, scales and slime with your fingers. Pat dry with paper towels.

  3. In the frying pan, combine the water, sake, sugar, mirin and sliced ginger and bring to the boil over medium heat.

  4. Add the fish skin side up in a single layer, with the green onion alongside, and spoon the liquid over it. When it returns to the boil, skim off any scum. Dissolve 1 tbsp of the miso in a ladle of the hot liquid and pour it in around the fish.

  5. Lay the drop lid on the fish and simmer over medium-low heat for 8 minutes. The liquid should bubble gently up around the edge of the lid.

  6. Remove the drop lid. Dissolve the remaining miso with the soy sauce in a little of the liquid and stir it into the sauce. Simmer uncovered over medium heat for 3–5 minutes, tilting the pan and spooning the sauce over the fish constantly, until it has reduced to a glossy glaze that coats the back of a spoon. Don't turn the fish.

  7. Serve the fish skin side up with the green onion, pour the sauce over and top with the drained ginger needles.

Notes

  • The fish is done when the flesh at the thickest part is opaque all the way through and flakes when pressed with chopsticks.
  • If the sauce gets too thick or starts to catch, add 1–2 tbsp water; if it's too thin, simmer for another 1–2 minutes. It thickens further as it cools.
  • Miso varies a lot. Red miso and Hatcho miso are saltier, so taste before adding the second half; white miso is sweeter and milder (see substitutions).
  • Mackerel fillets sometimes have a row of pin bones along the centre. Run a fingertip along the flesh and pull any out with tweezers before cooking.
  • Frozen fillets: thaw them overnight in the fridge, pat dry, then do the boiling-water step as usual.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
MackerelSardines (iwashi), Spanish mackerel (sawara) or yellowtail (buri) fillets; same method and timing
SakeDry white wine, or water for no alcohol
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
Awase or red misoWhite miso: use 3 tbsp in total and reduce the sugar to 2 tsp

Make ahead

This is better made ahead. Cook it completely the day before, let it cool in its sauce and refrigerate it so the flavour soaks right through. Reheat gently in a pan with 1–2 tbsp water, or covered in the microwave at 600 W for about 1½ minutes.

Storage

Keeps for 3 days in the fridge in its sauce. It freezes well for up to a month: put each piece in a freezer bag with its sauce, squeeze out the air and freeze flat. Thaw overnight in the fridge and reheat as above; it's also good cold in a bento.
