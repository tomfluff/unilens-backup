Chicken Karaage (Japanese Fried Chicken)

Juicy ginger-soy chicken thighs with a crackly potato-starch crust, double-fried like the karaage stalls at the Harukawa Lantern Festival.

If you walk along the Kagami River during the Harukawa Lantern Festival, you smell the karaage stalls before you see them. This year the festival is on Saturday 10 and Sunday 11 October, and I can already tell you where my family will be standing: in the queue at the stall run by the Hirose family from the old fish-market district, who have fried chicken in the same blackened wok for as long as I can remember. Sota calls it “lantern chicken” and eats it from a paper cone while the lanterns float down the river.

I spent years chasing that shatteringly crisp coating at home. Then one festival night Mr. Hirose took pity on me and shared his secret over the hiss of the oil: fry it twice, and let it rest in between. This is my home version, tested many times by Kenta, my recipe tester, and approved by a very strict nine-year-old.

Why you'll love it

  • Juicy inside and crackly outside, even an hour later, so it's a bento favourite.
  • Only six ingredients for the chicken itself, most of them already in your pantry.
  • The double fry takes out the guesswork: no more pale coating or pink middles.

Thighs, not breast

Use boneless chicken thighs with the skin on. Thigh meat has more fat, so it stays juicy through two rounds of frying, and the skin becomes the crispiest part. Cut each thigh into pieces of about 40 g (roughly 4 cm), as even as you can, so everything cooks at the same speed. If you only have breast, see the substitutions.

Potato starch and a little flour

Potato starch (katakuriko) gives that light, glassy, craggy crust, and a couple of spoons of flour help the coating stick and brown evenly. Coat the chicken just before frying, then squeeze each piece gently in your hand so the coating grips. If you can see wet patches, add a pinch more starch.

The double fry, explained

The first fry at 160°C (320°F) for 3 minutes cooks the chicken gently without burning the outside. The pieces then rest on a rack for 4 minutes while the heat carried inside keeps cooking the centre and the surface dries. The second, short fry at 180°C (350°F) for 1 minute drives off the last moisture and makes the coating crunchy. The centre should reach 75°C (167°F); a cheap probe thermometer takes away all the worry.

Tips for success

Pat the chicken dry before marinating, and take it out of the fridge 10 minutes before frying. Fry in two batches: crowding the pot drops the oil temperature and gives you greasy, pale chicken. And listen: the loud, wet bubbling turns into a lighter, crackling hiss when the pieces are ready. Leftovers reheat beautifully in a toaster oven, so make the full amount. For a festival-style feast at home, serve it with candied sweet potatoes and lantern dango.

Chicken Karaage (Japanese Fried Chicken)

Equipment

  • Heavy pot (20–22 cm) or wok for deep-frying
  • Deep-fry thermometer (or a probe thermometer)
  • Wire rack set over a tray
  • Long chopsticks or tongs, and a slotted spoon
  • Mixing bowl or zip-top bag for marinating

Instructions

  1. Pat the chicken dry with paper towels and trim off any excess yellow fat and bits of cartilage. Cut it into pieces of about 40 g (roughly 4 cm), 14–16 pieces in total, keeping the skin on.

  2. Mix the soy sauce, sake, sugar, ginger and garlic in a bowl or zip-top bag. Add the chicken and massage it for 1 minute, until most of the liquid has been absorbed. Refrigerate for 30 minutes (up to 12 hours).

  3. Take the chicken out 10 minutes before frying. Heat the oil, about 3 cm deep, to 160°C (320°F). Meanwhile, mix the potato starch and flour on a tray, tip off any marinade pooled around the chicken, and coat each piece, squeezing it gently in your hand and shaking off the excess.

  4. First fry: lower half the pieces into the oil one by one, skin side down. Leave them alone for the first minute so the coating sets, then turn them and fry for 3 minutes in total, until pale golden and firm and the bubbling has calmed down.

  5. Lift the pieces onto the wire rack and let them rest for 4 minutes; the heat inside keeps cooking the centre. Fry the second batch the same way while the first rests, keeping the oil at 160°C (320°F).

  6. Second fry: raise the heat and bring the oil to 180°C (350°F). Return the first batch and fry for 1 minute, turning, until deep golden brown and crisp, with small bubbles and a light, crackling sound. Repeat with the second batch.

  7. Drain on the rack, standing the pieces on their sides so the steam escapes. Check the thickest piece: 75°C (167°F) in the centre, or clear juices with no pink. Serve hot with lemon wedges and shredded cabbage.

Notes

  • The chicken is done when the centre of the thickest piece reads 75°C (167°F), or the juices run clear with no pink. If one is under, fry it for another 30–60 seconds at 170°C (340°F).
  • No thermometer? Dip dry wooden chopsticks in the oil: at 160°C small bubbles rise slowly from the tips; at 180°C lots of bubbles rush up straight away.
  • Don't marinate longer than 12 hours. The soy sauce draws out moisture and the chicken turns salty and firm, almost like ham.
  • If the coating falls off in the oil, the chicken was too wet or coated too early. Tip off the extra marinade, coat just before frying, and don't move the pieces for the first minute.
  • Strain the cooled oil through a paper filter and reuse it 2–3 times. When it darkens or smells, solidify it with an oil hardener and put it out with burnable rubbish (that's the rule in Harukawa).

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
SakeDry sherry or dry white wine, or water for no alcohol
Potato starchCornstarch; the coating is a little harder and crunchier
All-purpose flourMore potato starch, or rice flour (with gluten-free tamari instead of soy sauce, the dish becomes gluten-free)
Chicken thighsBoneless breast cut into 30 g pieces; shorten the first fry to 2½ minutes
Fresh ginger and garlic2 tsp tube ginger and 1 tsp tube garlic

Make ahead

Marinate the chicken the night before (up to 12 hours), but coat it only just before frying. For bento, fry the night before, cool on a rack uncovered, refrigerate, and reheat in the toaster oven in the morning until piping hot; let it cool before packing.

Storage

Keeps for 2 days in the fridge in a container lined with paper towel, or 3 weeks frozen in a freezer bag. Reheat in a toaster oven (about 1000 W) for 4–5 minutes, loosely covered with foil for the first 3 minutes; from frozen, microwave first at 600 W for 1 minute per 4 pieces. The microwave alone makes the coating soggy.
