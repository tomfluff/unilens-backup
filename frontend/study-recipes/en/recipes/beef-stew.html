Japanese-Style Beef Stew

Tender chunks of beef in a deep, glossy red wine sauce thickened with a homemade brown roux, the way my grandmother made it at her inn every November.

When the first frost touches the top of Mount Moonview, my grandmother Fumi used to say it was stew weather. Every November her little inn in Moonview Spa had a regular guest, a retired hotel cook from Kobe who came for the hot springs and the autumn leaves. Years ago he taught her his beef stew, and from then on a heavy pot of it sat at the back of her stove all afternoon on cold weekends. My job, aged about seven, was to stir the bottom every half hour. I still think of that whenever I lift the lid.

Japanese beef stew (bīfu shichū) is a yōshoku dish, close to a French daube but richer and a little sweeter: beef simmered in red wine until it falls apart, then finished with a brown roux, tomato, ketchup and Worcestershire sauce, so the sauce turns dark, glossy and thick enough to coat a spoon.

Why you'll love it

  • Most of the 2½ hours is hands-off simmering, on the hob or in the oven.
  • It tastes even better the next day, so it is perfect for guests.
  • The leftover sauce makes the best omurice or stew over rice.

Choosing the beef

Use a cut with plenty of connective tissue, which melts into gelatine and makes both the meat and the sauce silky. Chuck (kata-rōsu) is tender after about 2 hours. Shin (sune) is cheaper and even more gelatinous but needs 2½ to 3 hours. Avoid lean cuts like round (momo), which go dry and stringy however long you cook them. Buy a block and cut it into big 4–5 cm chunks yourself; small pieces shrink and fall to shreds.

The brown roux

This is the heart of the dish, and the step I ask you not to rush. Butter and flour are stirred over low-medium heat for 10 to 12 minutes until they turn the colour of milk chocolate and smell like toasted nuts. Stir constantly and scrape the corners. If you see black specks or smell anything burnt, start again; burnt roux makes the whole pot bitter, and 40 g of butter is a small price to pay.

Stovetop or oven

On the hob, keep the heat so low that the surface barely trembles and stir the bottom every 30 minutes. In the oven, the heat comes from all sides and nothing scorches: I put the lidded pot in at 160°C (325°F), or 140°C with a fan; see my oven temperature guide.

Tips for success

For the wine, choose something you would happily drink, nothing expensive. I use a fruity Muscat Bailey A from the Mirror valley. Add the salt at the very end, because the sauce reduces and concentrates. And when the stew is done, cool it quickly: divide it into shallow containers and refrigerate within two hours rather than leaving the big pot on the stove overnight.

Japanese-Style Beef Stew

Equipment

  • Heavy casserole or Dutch oven (about 24 cm, 4 L) with a lid
  • Small frying pan for the roux
  • Wooden spatula
  • Ladle

Instructions

  1. Pat the beef dry with kitchen paper, season it all over with the salt and pepper and leave it for 10 minutes while you slice the onions and prepare the vegetables.

  2. Heat the oil in the casserole over medium-high heat. Brown the beef in two batches without crowding, leaving each side alone for about 2 minutes, until every piece has a deep brown crust (6–8 minutes per batch). Lift the meat onto a plate.

  3. Turn the heat to medium, add the onions and 2 tbsp water and scrape up the brown bits. Cook, stirring often, for about 10 minutes until soft and amber. Add the garlic and tomato paste and cook for 1–2 minutes more, until the paste darkens to brick red.

  4. Return the beef and its juices to the pot, pour in the wine and boil hard over high heat for 3 minutes to cook off the alcohol and the raw sour taste. Add the water and bay leaf, bring back to the boil and skim off the grey foam.

  5. Cover with the lid slightly ajar and simmer on the lowest heat, with the surface barely trembling, for 90 minutes; stir the bottom every 30 minutes. Or put the lid on and cook in the oven at 160°C (325°F), 140°C with a fan, for 90 minutes.

  6. Meanwhile, make the roux. Melt the butter in a small frying pan over low-medium heat, add the flour and stir constantly with a wooden spatula for 10–12 minutes, until it is the colour of milk chocolate and smells of toasted nuts. Take it off the heat. If you see black specks, start again.

  7. Add the carrots and potatoes to the pot, with a little water if they are not quite covered. Cover and simmer for 25 minutes (on the hob or back in the oven), until a skewer slides easily through the potatoes and the beef.

  8. Set the pot over low heat on the hob. Ladle about 200 ml of the hot liquid into the roux pan a little at a time, stirring to a smooth paste (it will sputter at first, so stand back). Stir it into the pot with the ketchup, Worcestershire sauce, sugar and mushrooms. Simmer uncovered for 10 minutes, scraping the bottom, until the sauce coats the back of a spoon.

  9. Remove the bay leaf, taste and add salt (usually ¼–½ tsp). Serve in warm bowls with a swirl of cream and a little parsley, with rice, mashed potatoes or crusty bread.

Notes

  • The meat is ready when a chopstick slides in with almost no resistance. If it is still firm, it is undercooked, not overcooked: keep simmering in 15-minute stretches.
  • May Queen potatoes hold their shape. Floury Danshaku potatoes break up at the edges and thicken the sauce, which some people love.
  • Once the roux is in, the sauce catches easily. Keep the heat low and scrape the bottom of the pot as you stir.
  • Sauce too thin? Simmer it uncovered for a few minutes more. Too thick? Add water 50 ml at a time.
  • The sauce usually needs ¼ to ½ tsp extra salt at the end, but taste first; Worcestershire sauces vary a lot.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Red wineSame amount of beef stock (or water and 1 stock cube) plus 1 tbsp balsamic vinegar, for an alcohol-free stew
Homemade brown roux2 blocks (about 40 g) of store-bought demi-glace or hayashi roux, stirred in at step 8; leave out the sugar and taste before salting
Beef chuckPork shoulder in chunks; simmer 60 minutes instead of 90
Worcestershire sauceJapanese chūnō (medium-thick) sauce
All-purpose flour (gluten-free)Rice flour for the roux, cooked 5–6 minutes; it browns less, so add 1 tsp more tomato paste for colour

Make ahead

Make the whole stew a day ahead, cool it quickly and refrigerate. Next day, lift off the layer of set fat, then reheat gently with a splash of water, stirring the bottom.

Storage

Keeps 3 days in the fridge. To freeze, lift out the potatoes first (they turn spongy), then freeze for up to a month; thaw overnight in the fridge and reheat until bubbling all the way through.
