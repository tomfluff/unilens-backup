Japanese Hamburg Steak with Red Wine Sauce

Thick, juicy Japanese hamburg steak, seared and then steamed under a lid, with a glossy red wine and ketchup sauce made in the same pan.

Every family has a dinner that means "today is a good day", and in our house it is hamburg steak. When Hana brought home her first report card, I made hamburg. When Sota lost his first tooth, hamburg. Now that they are twelve and nine, they don't even tell me what they want on their birthdays; they just ask whether I've bought the mince yet. My mother made hers in Moonview Spa on Sunday evenings, and the smell of onions cooking in butter still takes me straight back to her kitchen.

If you have only met hamburgers in a bun, a Japanese hanbāgu is something different: a thick, soft oval patty, seared and then steamed under a lid so it stays tender, served with a glossy sauce made in the same pan. It is a yōshoku classic, the Western-style home cooking Japan made its own, and in my nine years as a school-lunch nutritionist it was the dish children cheered for.

Why you'll love it

  • It is juicy every time, thanks to three small habits explained below.
  • The red wine sauce takes three minutes in the same pan.
  • Raw patties freeze beautifully, so one batch gives you a second dinner.

Choosing the mince

Japanese supermarkets sell aibikiniku, mixed beef and pork mince. For hamburg I look for a 7:3 beef-to-pork mix: the beef brings flavour and the pork brings softness. At Okamoto, the butcher in the Springvale Ginza arcade, I ask for it ground once, not twice, so the texture stays meaty. If you only have all-beef mince, choose one with about 20% fat; very lean mince makes a dry patty however carefully you cook it.

Three habits for a juicy hamburg

Keep everything cold. The fat in mince starts to melt at about body temperature, and melted fat runs out into the pan instead of staying in the patty. The sautéed onion must be completely cool before it goes in, and I knead in a chilled bowl. If your hands run warm, dip them in iced water first.

Knead the meat with the salt first. Salt dissolves the meat proteins so they bind. About a minute of kneading turns the mince pale and sticky, with fine threads when you lift your hand. Only then add everything else. Under-kneaded patties crack in the pan and the juices escape.

Slap out the air and make a dent. Toss each ball from hand to hand five or six times, like clay. Trapped air expands and splits the patty. The dent is there because the centre puffs up as it cooks; with it, the patty ends up flat and cooks evenly.

Tips for success

Minced meat must reach 75°C in the centre, so if you own a thermometer, this is the dish to use it on. Otherwise push a skewer into the middle and look for clear juices, not pink. On cold evenings I serve this with kabocha soup, and if you need grams turned into cups, my measurement guide has you covered.

Japanese Hamburg Steak with Red Wine Sauce

Equipment

  • Large frying pan (about 26 cm) with a lid
  • Large mixing bowl, chilled
  • Tray or plate for chilling the patties
  • Instant-read thermometer (recommended)

Instructions

  1. Melt the 10 g butter in a frying pan over medium heat and cook the onion, stirring, for 6–8 minutes until soft and light golden. Spread it out on a plate and let it cool completely; 10 minutes in the fridge speeds this up. Warm onion would melt the fat in the meat.

  2. Put the panko in a small bowl, pour over the milk and leave it to soak for 5 minutes. Beat the egg in a cup.

  3. Put the mince and salt in a large chilled bowl and knead firmly with one hand for about 1 minute, until the meat turns paler and sticky and pulls into fine threads. Add the onion, soaked panko, egg, pepper and nutmeg, and mix for another 30 seconds, just until even.

  4. Divide the mixture into 4 (about 190 g each). With lightly oiled hands, toss each portion from hand to hand 5–6 times to knock out the air, then shape it into an oval about 2 cm thick and press a shallow dent into the centre. Lay them on a tray, cover and chill for 15 minutes so they hold their shape.

  5. Heat the oil in a large frying pan (26 cm) over medium heat. Add the patties dent side up and cook without moving them for 3 minutes, until the underside has a deep brown crust.

  6. Flip the patties, pour the 50 ml water around them, cover with the lid and turn the heat to low. Steam for 8–10 minutes. They are done when the centre reaches 75°C, or when a skewer pushed into the middle releases clear juices; if the juices are pink, give them 2 more minutes.

  7. Move the patties to warm plates. Wipe out the excess fat with kitchen paper but leave the brown bits. Add the red wine, turn the heat to medium-high and boil for 1–2 minutes, scraping the pan, until reduced by about half and the sharp alcohol smell has gone.

  8. Add the ketchup, Worcestershire sauce and sugar and simmer for about 1 minute, until glossy and slightly thick. Take the pan off the heat, swirl in the cold butter until melted and pour the sauce over the patties.

Notes

  • Cracks in the pan usually mean the mixture was under-kneaded or the onion was still warm. Keep the lid on and the patty will still cook through.
  • Salt is about 0.9% of the meat weight. Use the same ¾ tsp even with salted butter; the sauce brings the rest.
  • For thicker 3 cm patties, add 3–4 minutes to the steaming time and check the centre with a thermometer.
  • Sauce too sharp? Simmer it 30 seconds longer or add another ½ tsp sugar. Too thick? Stir in a spoonful of water.
  • Japanese-style (wafu) variation: skip the sauce and top each patty with grated daikon, a shiso leaf and a drizzle of ponzu.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Red wineSame amount of water plus 1 tsp soy sauce and an extra ½ tsp sugar (alcohol-free), or unsweetened grape juice
Mixed beef and pork minceAll-beef mince with about 20% fat; add 1 extra tbsp milk
Panko30 g fresh white bread (about half a thick slice), crusts removed and torn small
Worcestershire sauceJapanese chūnō (medium-thick) sauce; leave out the sugar
MilkSoy milk or water

Make ahead

Shape the patties (step 4), cover and keep them in the fridge for up to 24 hours. Or freeze them raw: wrap each patty in cling film, freeze flat for up to a month and thaw overnight in the fridge before cooking as usual.

Storage

Cooked patties keep for 2 days in the fridge, or up to 3 weeks frozen in their sauce. Reheat covered in the microwave at 600 W for about 2 minutes per patty, or simmer gently in the sauce with a lid until hot all the way through.
