Creamy Kabocha Soup

★★★★★ 4.7 from 244 ratings by Natsuki Aoyama Updated September 30, 2026 7 comments

A velvety, naturally sweet Japanese pumpkin soup with just kabocha, onion, butter and milk. Ready in 35 minutes, with an easy vegan version.

Sota will tell anyone who listens that he does not like vegetables. And yet every October he drinks two bowls of this soup and asks for a third. I learned that trick at school: in my nine years as a school-lunch nutritionist, kabocha potage was the one vegetable dish that came back to the kitchen with every bowl empty.

The kabocha I use comes from the Tsuchiya family's stall at the Saturday farmers' market by Harukawa Central. They grow it in the Kagami valley, below the yuzu orchards, and they harvest it in late summer. My grandmother Fumi used to line the kabocha up on the veranda of her inn in Tsukimi Onsen and leave them there for weeks, and she was right: kabocha gets sweeter after a month or two of storage, as its starch turns to sugar. So October and November are the sweetest months of all.

Why you'll love it

  • Six ingredients, no stock cube, and naturally gluten-free.
  • The microwave trick makes a hard kabocha easy and safe to cut.
  • You can freeze the purée and add the milk on the day.

Choosing a kabocha

Pick one that feels heavy for its size, with a dry, corky stem and dull, dark green skin. If you buy a cut wedge, look for deep orange flesh and plump, packed seeds. A whole kabocha keeps for a month or more somewhere cool and dry, but once cut, scoop out the seeds (that is where it starts to spoil), wrap it and use it within a week. Find out what else is in season in my seasonal produce guide.

Cutting it safely

A raw kabocha is hard enough to trap a knife. Microwave the wedge at 600 W for 2 to 3 minutes first, until the skin gives a little when pressed. Put it flat side down on the board and cut with steady pressure from the top, keeping your other hand flat on the back of the blade, never in front of it. My knife cuts guide shows the grip.

Tips for success

I peel the kabocha completely for a bright golden soup; if you leave some skin on, the soup turns khaki green, still delicious but less pretty. Sweat the onion gently without browning it. Cook the kabocha in water first and add the milk only after blending, then heat it gently without boiling, because boiled milk can separate and form a skin.

Hot soup in a blender needs care. Steam builds up and can blow the lid off. Let the soup cool for 5 minutes, fill the jug no more than halfway, take out the centre cap, cover the hole with a folded tea towel and start on the lowest speed. A stick blender used off the heat is even easier.

Kabocha varies a lot in sweetness. If yours tastes flat, add a teaspoon of sugar or honey; if it is watery, simmer the soup a few minutes longer before the milk goes in. I serve it with warm milk bread for an easy Sunday lunch.

Creamy Kabocha Soup

Ingredients

Equipment

  • Medium pot (about 20 cm) with a lid
  • Blender or stick blender
  • Sharp, heavy knife and a large board
  • Microwave-safe plate

Instructions

  1. Put the kabocha on a microwave-safe plate and microwave at 600 W for 2–3 minutes, until the skin gives slightly when pressed. Scoop out any remaining seeds, peel it, and cut the flesh into 1 cm thick slices.

  2. Melt the butter in the pot over medium-low heat. Add the onion and a pinch of the salt and cook gently, stirring, for about 5 minutes until soft and translucent but not browned.

  3. Add the kabocha and stir for 1 minute to coat it in butter. Pour in the water, add the rest of the salt and bring to the boil. Cover and simmer on low heat for 12–15 minutes, until the kabocha crumbles easily when pressed against the side of the pot.

  4. Take the pot off the heat and let it cool for 5 minutes. Blend until completely smooth: with a stick blender in the pot, or in a jug blender filled no more than halfway, centre cap removed and the hole covered with a folded tea towel, starting on the lowest speed.

  5. Return the purée to the pot, stir in the milk and warm it over low heat, stirring, until steaming hot but not boiling (about 3 minutes). Thin it with a little more milk or water if you like, then taste and add salt.

  6. Ladle into warm bowls and finish with a swirl of cream, a grind of black pepper and a few toasted pumpkin seeds.

Notes

  • The kabocha is cooked when a piece crumbles easily against the side of the pot with a spatula.
  • For an extra-silky soup, push it through a fine sieve after blending.
  • Thickness: add milk or water 50 ml at a time until it coats a spoon but still pours easily.
  • The salt starts light on purpose. Taste at the end; most kabocha needs about another ¼ tsp, and a cold soup needs a little more than a hot one.
  • Toasted pumpkin seeds make a nice topping: rinse the seeds, dry them and toast them in a dry frying pan over medium-low heat for 5–6 minutes until they pop.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Butter (vegan)1 tbsp olive oil or vegetable oil
Milk (vegan)Unsweetened soy milk or oat milk; heat it gently, as soy milk curdles if it boils. Add ½ tsp white miso for depth.
KabochaButternut squash; it is less sweet and more watery, so use 600 g and simmer uncovered for the last 5 minutes
Onion1 leek, white part, sliced

Make ahead

Make the soup up to the end of step 4 (blended, before the milk) up to 2 days ahead and refrigerate. On the day, reheat the purée, then add the milk and warm it through.

Storage

With milk, keep it in the fridge for 2 days. To freeze, freeze the purée before adding the milk, for up to a month; thaw overnight in the fridge, reheat and stir in the milk. Soup frozen with milk can turn grainy.
