Daigaku-imo (Candied Sweet Potatoes with Black Sesame)

Crisp-fried chunks of Japanese sweet potato tossed in a thin, glossy soy-sugar syrup and black sesame. A Lantern Festival favourite, with an easy cold-oil method if deep-frying makes you nervous.

Autumn in Springvale smells of three things: the yuzu orchards, the roasted-chestnut carts outside Springvale Central station, and sweet potatoes. From late September the sandy fields along the edge of Harvest Bay are dug up, and the farmers bring their muddy, purple-skinned potatoes to the morning market by the crateful. The Hamada family's stall sells them for 300 yen a bag and always slips in an extra one for Sota, who has never once said no to daigaku-imo.

Daigaku-imo means 'university potatoes'. The most common story is that they were sold near universities in Tokyo in the early twentieth century and were cheap and filling enough for hungry students. Whatever the truth, here they are a festival classic: at the Lantern Festival there is always a stall selling paper cups of them, glistening and sprinkled with black sesame, between the dango and the karaage. My grandmother Fumi served them at her inn as a teatime snack after a soak in the hot spring, and they were the first thing I ever learned to fry.

Why you'll love it

  • Crisp outside, fluffy inside, with a thin glossy syrup that crackles.
  • A handful of ingredients, and the cold-oil method needs very little attention.
  • Vegan, and as good at room temperature as warm.

Choosing the sweet potatoes

Look for Japanese sweet potatoes with purple-red skin and pale yellow flesh. Naruto Kintoki and Beni Azuma are fluffy and hold their shape, which is ideal. Beni Haruka and Silk Sweet are sweeter and moister, almost creamy, and can turn soft, so cut them a little bigger. Choose firm, heavy potatoes without soft spots. The orange-fleshed sweet potatoes sold as 'yams' abroad are too wet for this. Cut them into chunky rangiri wedges (see my knife cuts guide) so every piece has plenty of edges to catch the syrup.

Frying without fear

If deep-frying makes you nervous, try the cold-oil method: put the potatoes in a frying pan, pour in oil to come halfway up, and only then turn on the heat. Nothing splashes as they go in, you use less oil, and the slow rise in temperature makes the inside especially sweet, because the potato's own enzymes turn starch into sugar most actively between about 60 and 75°C. For the classic method, a thermometer helps: 160–170°C (320–340°F).

Tips for success

Soak the cut potatoes for 10 minutes and dry them very well. The syrup is the only tricky part: boil it just until the bubbles get bigger and slower, about 2 minutes. Boil it longer and it sets like hard candy as it cools. Having a festival night at home? Serve them with lantern dango, and for another autumn sweet, try my kabocha pudding.

Daigaku-imo (Candied Sweet Potatoes with Black Sesame)

Equipment

  • Frying pan, 24–26 cm, for the cold-oil method; or a deep pot and a thermometer for deep-frying
  • Long chopsticks or tongs, and a bamboo skewer to test doneness
  • Wire rack or a tray lined with kitchen paper
  • Baking paper for cooling

Instructions

  1. Scrub the sweet potatoes and leave the skin on; it adds colour and helps the pieces hold together. Trim both ends and cut into rolling wedges (rangiri) about 3–4 cm long.

  2. Soak the pieces in cold water for 10 minutes, changing the water once if it turns cloudy. This washes off surface starch so they don't stick together or darken. Drain and pat them completely dry with a clean towel.

  3. Cold-oil method: lay the potatoes in a single layer in the frying pan and pour in oil to come halfway up them (about 1.5 cm). Turn the heat to medium. Once the oil is bubbling steadily, after about 5 minutes, cook for 10 minutes more, turning now and then, until a skewer slides through the biggest piece.

  4. Deep-fry method (instead of step 3): heat about 700 ml oil in a deep pot to 160–170°C (320–340°F); a dry chopstick dipped in should give off a steady stream of small bubbles. Fry the potatoes for 7–8 minutes, turning, until a skewer slides through.

  5. For either method, turn the heat up to high (about 180°C/350°F) for the last minute, until the surface is golden and crisp. Lift the potatoes onto a rack or kitchen paper to drain.

  6. Pour the oil out of the frying pan (or use a clean one) and wipe it out. Add the sugar, water, mirin and soy sauce and set over medium heat. Don't stir; swirl the pan instead. Once it boils, cook for about 2 minutes, until the bubbles become bigger and pop more slowly and the syrup is as thick as warm honey. Don't let it brown.

  7. Turn off the heat, add the potatoes and toss quickly to coat every piece. Sprinkle in the black sesame and toss again.

  8. Spread the pieces out on baking paper, not touching, and leave for 5 minutes. The syrup sets into a glossy, slightly crisp coating. Serve warm or at room temperature.

Notes

  • Doneness test: a bamboo skewer should slide into the thickest piece with no resistance before you crisp them up.
  • If the syrup turns hard and glassy, it boiled too long. Return the pan to low heat with 1 tbsp water and stir until it melts, then toss again. If it is runny and won't cling, boil it 30 seconds more.
  • A few drops of rice vinegar (about ¼ tsp) in the syrup help stop it crystallising if your kitchen is cold or you are doubling the batch.
  • Oil safety: dry the potatoes well so the oil doesn't spit, never leave hot oil unattended, and let it cool completely before straining it for reuse.
  • Oven version (no frying): toss the potatoes with 2 tbsp oil and bake on baking paper at 200°C (400°F), or 180°C for a fan oven, for 25–30 minutes, turning once, then toss them in the syrup.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
SugarReplace up to half with honey; the syrup stays softer and stickier
Black sesameWhite sesame, or chopped toasted walnuts
Japanese sweet potatoesKorean goguma or any purple-skinned, pale-fleshed sweet potato; orange-fleshed 'yams' turn mushy

Make ahead

Fry the potatoes up to 4 hours ahead and leave them uncovered on a rack; make the syrup and toss just before serving. Finished daigaku-imo also pack well into a bento the next day.

Storage

Keeps for 2 days in an airtight container in the fridge. The syrup goes firm and slightly crunchy when cold, which plenty of people (Sota included) love; to soften, microwave at 600 W for 20–30 seconds. Freezes for a month, spread out so the pieces don't stick; thaw at room temperature.
