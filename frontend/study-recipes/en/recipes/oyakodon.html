Oyakodon (Chicken and Egg Rice Bowl)

Tender chicken thigh and onion simmered in a sweet dashi and soy broth, then set with softly cooked egg and slid over hot rice. One small pan, 25 minutes.

At my grandmother's inn in Tsukimi Onsen there was a quiet hour every day, after the morning guests had checked out and before the afternoon guests arrived. That was when the staff ate, and more often than not Fumi made oyakodon. She cooked each bowl in a little copper pan with an upright handle, sliding the trembling egg onto the rice with a flick of her wrist, and I sat on a stool in the corner of the kitchen waiting for mine. I have that pan now. Hana, who is twelve, borrowed it last spring and made her first oyakodon by herself, and it has been her Saturday lunch ever since.

Oyako means "parent and child", the chicken and the egg, and don is short for donburi, a rice bowl. Chicken thigh and sliced onion simmer for a few minutes in dashi, soy sauce and mirin, then beaten egg goes in and sets just enough to hold together. The whole thing slides over hot rice, and the sauce seeps down into it.

Why you'll love it

  • It's on the table in 25 minutes from a fridge of basics.
  • One small pan and a bowl are all the washing up.
  • The broth ratio is easy to remember: 5 parts dashi, 1 soy sauce, 1 mirin.

One bowl at a time

Cook each serving separately in a small pan, 16 to 20 cm across. It seems fussy, but in a big pan the egg spreads thin and overcooks at the edges while the middle is still raw. A small frying pan with a lid works perfectly; you don't need a special oyakodon pan. Make the dashi from scratch if you have time, or use granules; both are delicious here.

The secret is two pours

Beat the eggs only lightly, so you can still see streaks of white. Pour about two-thirds over the simmering chicken first. When the edges begin to set, pour the rest into the centre, put the lid on and turn off the heat. The first pour cooks through and holds everything together; the second stays soft and glossy. That contrast is what makes a great oyakodon.

A note on soft eggs, for readers abroad

In Japan, eggs are produced to be eaten raw, so a soft, barely set top is normal and safe. Many countries' eggs are not. If yours are not pasteurised or salmonella-controlled, or you are cooking for someone pregnant, very young, elderly or with a weak immune system, leave the lid on over low heat for 2 to 3 minutes until the egg is fully set. The chicken must always be cooked through to 75°C, whatever you do with the egg.

Tips for success

Cut the chicken into even 2–3 cm pieces and slice through the thickest parts so they cook at the same speed. Make sure the rice is piping hot before the egg goes in, because the egg waits for no one. For more autumn rice, try my mushroom rice.

Oyakodon (Chicken and Egg Rice Bowl)

Ingredients

For the broth

Quantities in the instructions are for the original recipe.

Equipment

  • Small frying pan (16–20 cm) with a lid, or an oyakodon pan
  • Two deep rice bowls
  • Chopsticks for beating the egg
  • Measuring jug

Instructions

  1. Make sure the rice is hot. Trim any large lumps of fat from the chicken and cut it into even 2–3 cm pieces, slicing through the thickest parts. Slice the onion 5 mm thick and cut the mitsuba into 3 cm lengths.

  2. Mix the dashi, soy sauce, mirin and sugar. Crack the eggs into a bowl and beat them lightly, cutting through the whites with chopsticks 10–15 times; stop while you can still see streaks of white.

  3. For the first bowl, put half the broth and half the onion into the small pan and bring to the boil over medium heat. Add half the chicken, skin side down, in one layer. Simmer for 3 minutes, turn the pieces and simmer for 3 minutes more, until the chicken is white all the way through and the onion is soft.

  4. Keep the broth bubbling over medium heat. Take half the beaten egg for this bowl and pour about two-thirds of it in a spiral from the outside in. Leave it for 30–40 seconds, until the edges start to set and puff.

  5. Pour the rest of this bowl's egg into the centre, scatter over half the mitsuba, cover with the lid and turn off the heat. Leave for 30 seconds to 1 minute, until the top is just set but still glossy. (For a fully set egg, keep it on low heat with the lid on for 2–3 minutes.)

  6. Tilt the pan over a bowl of hot rice and slide the chicken and egg on top with a spatula, pouring over any remaining broth. Repeat steps 3–5 for the second bowl. Sprinkle with shichimi if you like and eat straight away.

Notes

  • To check the chicken, cut the largest piece in half: it should be white all the way through with clear juices (75°C).
  • For a firmer egg, keep the heat on low with the lid on for 2–3 minutes after the second pour.
  • The broth ratio of 5:1:1 (dashi, soy sauce, mirin) scales easily; 75 ml dashi + 1 tbsp each makes one bowl.
  • For one serving, use half of everything and 2 eggs (1½ eggs is hard to measure; the extra egg is fine).
  • Same method, other toppings: thin beef or pork instead of chicken makes tanindon, the "strangers' bowl".

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Dashi½ tsp instant dashi granules dissolved in 150 ml hot water
Chicken thighChicken breast, sliced 1 cm thick and simmered 4–5 minutes; it is leaner and less juicy
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar for each tbsp of mirin (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
MitsubaThinly sliced green onion or chives
Soy sauce (gluten-free)Tamari

Make ahead

Cut the chicken and onion and mix the sauce up to a day ahead and keep them in the fridge. You can also simmer the chicken and onion in the sauce (step 3), chill it, then bring it back to a bubble and add the egg just before eating.

Storage

Oyakodon is best eaten at once. If you must keep some, cook the egg until fully set, store the topping apart from the rice in the fridge for up to 1 day and reheat until piping hot. It does not freeze well; the egg turns spongy and watery.
