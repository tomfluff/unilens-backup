Ginger Pork (Shogayaki)

Thin slices of pork loin seared and glazed in a sweet-salty soy and fresh ginger sauce, piled next to crisp shredded cabbage. Dinner in 20 minutes.

Daisuke and I met at Harukawa University, where I was studying nutrition and he was supposed to be studying engineering. Most evenings we ended up at Shokudō Kamome, a tiny diner by the east gate where the ginger pork set meal cost 580 yen and came with all the rice you could eat. Twenty years later, when Daisuke has had a long day, he doesn't ask for anything fancy. He asks for shogayaki, "like Kamome".

Shōgayaki means "ginger grill", and it is one of the great Japanese weeknight dinners: thin slices of pork, seared quickly and tossed in a glossy sauce of soy sauce, mirin, sake and plenty of freshly grated ginger. With a mound of shredded cabbage, a bowl of rice and miso soup, it is the classic teishoku set meal, and from fridge to table it takes about twenty minutes.

Why you'll love it

  • It's fast: the pork cooks in about 3 minutes.
  • The flour trick gives you a sauce that clings instead of pooling.
  • Leftovers are wonderful cold, in a bento.

Choosing the pork

Japanese supermarkets sell pork loin sliced especially for this dish, labelled shōgayaki-yō, about 4–5 mm thick. Abroad, ask the butcher to slice it for you, or put a piece of loin in the freezer for 30 to 40 minutes until firm and slice it yourself. Pork shoulder (kata-rōsu) has more fat marbled through it, so it stays juicier and is harder to overcook; it is what Kamome used, I am almost sure.

The ginger

Use fresh ginger and grate it with the skin on: most of the fragrance sits just under the skin. Keep all the juice, which is where the heat is. In autumn the shops have mature, stored ginger (hine shōga), which is pungent and fibrous. New ginger (shin shōga), sold in early summer with pink tips, is milder, so use about half as much again.

Here is the one rule: don't marinate the pork for long. Fresh ginger contains an enzyme that breaks down meat protein. Ten or fifteen minutes softens the surface nicely, but leave it overnight and the pork turns mealy and mushy. That is why I add the sauce at the end, in the pan, which keeps the meat tender and the ginger bright.

Tips for success

Make a few small cuts through the white sinew between the fat and the meat, so the slices don't curl up in the pan. Dust the pork lightly with flour just before cooking: it browns better, stays moist and holds the sauce like a glaze. And turn the heat down to medium before the sauce goes in, because the mirin and sugar caramelise in seconds. A fluffy tamagoyaki on the side makes it a feast, and my pantry staples guide explains mirin and sake.

Ginger Pork (Shogayaki)

Equipment

  • Large frying pan (about 26 cm)
  • Fine grater for the ginger
  • Tea strainer or small sieve for dusting flour
  • Bowl of cold water for the cabbage

Instructions

  1. Shred the cabbage as finely as you can (about 2 mm), soak it in cold water for 5 minutes to crisp it up, then drain well and spin or pat dry. Pile it onto two plates.

  2. Grate the ginger, skin and all, and mix it and its juice with the soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar in a small bowl until the sugar dissolves.

  3. Pat the pork dry. Make 3–4 short cuts (about 1 cm) through the white sinew between the fat and the meat on each slice so it doesn't curl. Just before cooking, dust both sides thinly with the flour through a tea strainer and shake off the excess.

  4. Heat the oil in the frying pan over medium-high heat. Add the onion and cook for 2 minutes until the edges soften, then push it to the side. Lay the pork slices flat without overlapping (in two batches if they don't fit) and cook for about 1 minute per side, until lightly browned and no longer pink.

  5. Turn the heat down to medium, give the sauce a stir and pour it over the pork. Toss everything for 1–2 minutes, until the sauce bubbles, thickens and coats the meat with a glossy glaze, with a spoonful still left in the pan.

  6. Arrange the pork and onion next to the cabbage and spoon the pan sauce over the meat. Serve straight away with rice, miso soup, and mayonnaise and tomato on the side if you like.

Notes

  • Never marinate the pork in the ginger sauce for more than 15 minutes; the ginger enzyme makes it mushy.
  • The pork is done when no pink remains in the thickest slice. Thin slices reach a safe temperature in a minute or two, so don't keep cooking them in the sauce.
  • Shake off the excess flour; a thick coat turns gluey in the sauce.
  • If the sauce reduces too far and starts to catch, add 1–2 tbsp water and shake the pan.
  • For a sharper ginger kick, stir 1 tsp extra grated ginger in just before serving.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Pork loinThinly sliced pork shoulder (juicier) or pork belly slices (richer)
Fresh ginger2 tsp ginger paste from a tube; it is milder, so add a little more if you like it hot
Mirin1 tbsp sake + 1 tsp sugar for each tbsp of mirin (alcohol-free: 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar)
SakeDry sherry or dry white wine, or water for no alcohol
All-purpose flour (gluten-free)Potato starch, with tamari instead of soy sauce

Make ahead

Mix the sauce and shred the cabbage in the morning; keep the cabbage in a container lined with damp kitchen paper in the fridge. Dust the pork with flour only just before cooking, and don't let it sit in the sauce.

Storage

Keeps 2 days in the fridge; eat it cold in a bento or reheat covered in the microwave at 600 W for about 1 minute. It freezes in its sauce for 2 weeks, though the meat gets a little firmer. Keep the cabbage separate.
