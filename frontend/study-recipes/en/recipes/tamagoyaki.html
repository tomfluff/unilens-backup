Tamagoyaki (Japanese Rolled Omelette)

★★★★★ 4.7 from 538 ratings by Natsuki Aoyama Updated September 12, 2026 7 comments
Jump to Recipe

A lightly sweet rolled omelette made in thin layers with a splash of dashi. Ten minutes, four eggs, and the star of every bento box.

Every school morning at 6:15, before anyone else is up, I make one tamagoyaki. It goes into two bento boxes, Hana's and Sota's, and the two scruffy end pieces go into me with my first coffee. I've made this rolled omelette something like three thousand times, and I still love the moment when the last layer rolls over and the whole thing becomes a neat golden log.

October is sports-day season in Japan, and on the morning of undōkai the whole of Springvale seems to be making tamagoyaki at once. My grandmother Fumi made a softer, dashi-rich version for breakfast at her inn in Moonview Spa, served with a little mound of grated daikon. My recipe sits halfway between hers and the sweet bento style: lightly sweet, juicy from a little dashi, but sturdy enough to sit in a lunchbox until noon.

Why you'll love it

  • Four eggs, ten minutes, and ingredients you already have.
  • Lovely warm for breakfast and just as good at room temperature in a bento.
  • Once you get the rolling rhythm, you'll never need the recipe again.

Sweet or savoury?

Tamagoyaki in eastern Japan tends to be sweet, while Kansai-style dashimaki tamago is savoury and very juicy, with up to 6 tbsp of dashi for 4 eggs. My 2 tsp of sugar gives a gentle sweetness. For a sweeter omelette go up to 1 tbsp, but turn the heat down a little, because sugar browns quickly. To try dashimaki, add more dashi gradually: the more liquid, the softer the egg and the trickier it is to roll.

The pan

A square tamagoyaki pan (mine is 13 × 18 cm) makes the neatest rolls, but a 20 cm round frying pan works too: as you roll, fold the curved sides in to make a rectangle. Non-stick is easiest. Professionals use copper, but I wouldn't start there.

Tips for success

Don't whisk the eggs into a foam. Cut through the whites with chopsticks, keeping the tips on the bottom of the bowl, so you don't beat in air. Medium heat, and oil every layer: I keep a folded paper towel soaked in oil in a small dish and wipe the pan each time. The egg should sizzle the moment it touches the pan. Pop the big bubbles with the tip of a chopstick so you don't get holes. And roll while the top is still slightly runny; that wet egg is the glue that holds the layers together. If the pan gets too hot, lift it off the flame for a few seconds rather than fiddling with the knob.

Don't panic if the first layer looks messy. It ends up in the middle and nobody will ever see it. For bento, make sure the egg is cooked through and completely cool before packing. It sits beautifully next to karaage and a mushroom rice onigiri.

Tamagoyaki (Japanese Rolled Omelette)

Equipment

  • Square tamagoyaki pan (about 13 × 18 cm) or a 20 cm non-stick frying pan
  • Long chopsticks and a thin spatula
  • Folded paper towel in a small dish of oil, for greasing
  • Bamboo sushi mat (makisu) or paper towel, for shaping
  • Fine sieve (optional, for an extra-smooth omelette)

Instructions

  1. Crack the eggs into a bowl. Beat them with chopsticks, keeping the tips on the bottom of the bowl and cutting through the whites about 30 times, without making foam. Add the dashi, sugar, mirin, soy sauce and salt and mix until the sugar dissolves. For an extra-smooth finish, pour the mixture through a sieve.

  2. Heat the pan over medium heat for about 1 minute, then wipe it with the oiled paper towel. Test with a drop of egg: it should sizzle immediately and set within a second or two.

  3. Pour in about a quarter of the egg (about 60 ml) and tilt the pan so it covers the base. Pop any big bubbles with the tip of a chopstick.

  4. When the edges have set but the top is still slightly runny, after 30–40 seconds, roll the egg from the far side towards you in 3 or 4 turns, using chopsticks or the spatula.

  5. Push the roll back to the far side and oil the empty part of the pan, lifting the roll to oil underneath it too. Pour in another quarter of the egg and lift the roll so the egg flows underneath. Pop the bubbles and, when it is half set, roll towards you again. Repeat until all the egg is used (4 layers). If it browns too fast, lift the pan off the heat for a few seconds.

  6. After the last roll, press the omelette against the sides of the pan with the spatula for about 10 seconds on each side to square it off. Turn it out onto a bamboo mat or paper towel, wrap it up firmly and leave it for 5 minutes to set its shape.

  7. Cut into 8 slices about 2 cm thick. Serve warm with a little grated daikon and a drop of soy sauce, or cool completely before packing in a bento.

Notes

  • It's cooked when the roll feels springy when you press it and no liquid egg seeps out when you slice it. For bento, it must be cooked through.
  • If you go up to 1 tbsp sugar, cook over medium-low heat; sugary egg burns quickly.
  • If the layers separate when you slice it, you rolled too late, once the top had dried. Roll sooner, while the surface is still glossy.
  • Fillings: 2 tbsp finely sliced green onion or shirasu mixed into the egg, or a half sheet of nori laid on a layer before rolling.
  • In a 20 cm round pan, cook the egg in 3 layers instead of 4 and fold the curved edges in each time.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
DashiWater plus an extra pinch of salt, or ¼ tsp instant dashi granules dissolved in 3 tbsp hot water
Mirin1 tsp sake + ⅓ tsp sugar, or leave it out and add an extra ½ tsp sugar
Light soy sauceRegular soy sauce; the omelette will be slightly darker
Square panA 20 cm round frying pan; fold the curved sides in as you roll

Make ahead

For bento, you can cook the omelette the night before: cool it completely, wrap it tightly in cling film, refrigerate it, and slice it in the morning. The egg mixture can be mixed up to a few hours ahead and kept in the fridge.

Storage

Keeps for 2 days in the fridge, wrapped in cling film. It freezes for up to 2 weeks with each slice wrapped separately; the texture becomes slightly spongy. Reheat frozen slices in the microwave at 600 W for about 20 seconds per slice until hot, and cool them before packing a bento.
