Kabocha Pudding with Caramel

Silky Japanese kabocha purin baked in a water bath under a layer of bittersweet caramel. With an oven method, a frying-pan method, and how to avoid the little holes.

For nine years I planned school lunches at an elementary school here in Springvale, and there was exactly one day each year when not a single child left anything on the tray: kabocha pudding day, at the end of October. The kitchen staff steamed hundreds of little cups of it, and the whole corridor smelled of caramel. When I left the job, the first recipe I asked the head cook for was that one, and this is my home version, a little richer, with cream.

It also reminds me of my grandmother Fumi, who made plain custard purin for the guests at her inn in Moonview Spa in a big wooden steamer, because she never owned an oven. That's why there's a frying-pan method below. These days Hana makes the caramel with me (under close supervision), and Sota is in charge of turning the puddings out onto plates, which he does with a lot of ceremony.

Why you'll love it

  • Smooth and dense, with a real kabocha flavour, not just a hint.
  • Naturally gluten-free, and you can make it two days ahead.
  • Works in the oven or in a frying pan with a lid.

Making the caramel

Caramel is just sugar and a spoonful of water cooked until it turns deep amber, about the colour of strong black tea. Don't stir it, just swirl the pan; stirring makes it crystallise. Too pale and it tastes only sweet; too dark, with black smoke, and it's bitter. Then comes the one moment to be careful: adding the hot water makes the caramel spit and steam violently. Wear an oven glove, hold the pan away from you, and keep children back. Hot water spatters much less than cold.

Avoiding holes (su)

If your pudding comes out with little holes and a spongy texture, it got too hot. In Japanese we call this su, the same word as for the holes in an overcooked daikon. Custard sets smoothly at around 80°C and turns holey above about 90°C. So bake it gently at 160°C (320°F), or 140°C (285°F) in a fan oven, in a water bath, with foil over each ramekin. Holey pudding still tastes good, but it's worth getting right.

Tips for success

Choose a heavy, dense kabocha; the drier and sweeter it is, the better the pudding. Microwaving it takes 5–6 minutes at 600 W (for 500 W, multiply by 1.2), and the skin cuts away easily while it's hot. For a bright yellow pudding remove all the skin; a little left on gives green flecks and a deeper flavour. Strain the mixture, twice if you have the patience; it's what makes it silky.

Equipment

  • 6 heatproof ramekins or pudding cups, about 130 ml each (7–8 cm across)
  • Small saucepan for the caramel
  • Fine-mesh sieve
  • Deep oven tray or roasting tin (at least 3 cm deep) for the water bath
  • Aluminium foil
  • Oven glove
  • Or, for the stovetop method: a deep frying pan (26 cm) with a lid

Instructions

  1. Cut the kabocha into 3–4 cm chunks and put them in a heatproof bowl with 1 tbsp water. Cover loosely with cling film and microwave at 600 W for 5–6 minutes, until a skewer slides through easily. While it's hot, hold each piece with a cloth and cut off the skin; you need 200 g of flesh.

  2. Make the caramel: put the 50 g sugar and 1 tbsp water in a small pan over medium heat. Don't stir. When it starts to colour at the edges, swirl the pan gently, and cook until it is a deep amber, like strong black tea, with a wisp of smoke. Take it off the heat, put on an oven glove, and at arm's length add the 2 tbsp hot water; it will spit and steam hard. Swirl to combine, then quickly pour into the 6 ramekins (about 2 tsp each) and tilt to cover the bottoms. It sets as it cools.

  3. Preheat the oven to 160°C (320°F), or 140°C (285°F) for a fan oven, and boil the kettle. Line a deep oven tray or roasting tin with a folded kitchen towel or two sheets of kitchen paper (it stops the ramekins sliding and softens the heat from below) and set the ramekins on it.

  4. Mash the hot kabocha with the 50 g sugar until completely smooth, using a fork or masher, or a hand blender for the smoothest result. Taste it, and add a little more sugar if it's bland. Stir in the eggs one at a time, gently, so you don't beat in air.

  5. Warm the milk and cream together until steaming, about 60°C, with small bubbles at the edge but not boiling (or microwave at 600 W for about 2 minutes). Pour it into the kabocha mixture a little at a time, stirring, and add the vanilla.

  6. Strain the mixture through a fine sieve, pressing it through with a spatula; strain it a second time for the silkiest texture. Skim off any foam with a spoon, or lay a sheet of kitchen paper on the surface and lift it away.

  7. Pour the mixture gently over the set caramel and cover each ramekin with a square of foil. Put the tray in the oven and pour in hot water (about 70°C; half boiling, half cold works) to 2 cm deep. Bake for 35–40 minutes.

  8. Check one: give the ramekin a gentle shake. It's done when the surface wobbles as one piece, like jelly, with no liquid ripples in the centre. If the middle is still runny, bake 5 minutes more. Little holes on the sides mean it got too hot; lower the oven by 10°C next time.

  9. No oven? Use a deep frying pan with a lid instead of steps 3 and 7: lay a cloth or kitchen paper in the pan, set the foil-covered ramekins on it and add hot water to 2 cm deep. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, cover, and cook on the lowest heat for 15 minutes; the water should only shiver with tiny bubbles. Turn off the heat and leave covered for 10 minutes more.

  10. Lift the ramekins out of the water and let them cool to room temperature, about 30 minutes. Cover and chill for at least 4 hours, ideally overnight.

  11. To unmould, press around the edge of the pudding with a fingertip (or run a thin knife around it) to let air in. Put a plate upside down on top, turn the ramekin over and give it one or two firm shakes; the pudding drops out with the caramel flowing over it. If it sticks, dip the bottom of the ramekin in hot water for 10 seconds.

Notes

  • Holes (su) mean the custard got hotter than about 90°C: the oven was too hot, the water bath boiled, or there was no foil. Next time lower the oven by 10°C and make sure the bath water is hot but not boiling.
  • Hot caramel is around 170°C and sticks to skin. Add the hot water at arm's length, never lean over the pan, and keep children away. If the caramel goes black and acrid, start again; it's only 50 g of sugar.
  • To clean the caramel pan, fill it with water and bring it to a boil; the hardened caramel dissolves.
  • Kabocha sweetness varies a lot. Taste the mashed kabocha with the sugar before adding the eggs, and add up to 10 g more sugar if it tastes bland.
  • The pudding is done when it wobbles as one piece, like jelly, with no liquid ripples in the centre.

Substitutions

Instead ofUse
Heavy creamMore milk (300 ml milk in total); lighter, with a softer set
KabochaCanned pumpkin purée (not pie filling), 200 g, plus 10 g extra sugar; or steamed sweet potato
Milk and creamUnsweetened soy milk and soy or oat cream, for a dairy-free version
Sugar in the custardLight brown sugar or kibi sugar, for a deeper, toffee-like flavour

Make ahead

This is a perfect make-ahead dessert: make it up to 2 days before, keep it covered in the fridge in the ramekins, and turn it out just before serving. Overnight, the caramel turns into a runny sauce.

Storage

Keeps 3 days covered in the fridge. It doesn't freeze well as a pudding (it turns watery and grainy when thawed), although my kids like it eaten half-frozen, like ice cream.
