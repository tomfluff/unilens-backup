    Kenzō Hattori (服部健三, Hattori Kenzō;[a] 2 February 1873 – 9 June 1941) was a Japanese railway engineer from Springvale who planned and built the Mirror Line of the Springvale Railway. As chief engineer of the Mirror Valley Railway, the company now called the Springvale Railway, from 1923 he laid out the 38.6 km route up the valley of the Mirror River to Moonview Spa and designed the Futamata Bridge (1929) and the 2,184 m Moonview Tunnel (1931). He was president of the company from 1931 to 1938. He is remembered for insisting on a ruling gradient of 25 ‰ even though it required the long tunnel.[1]

    Early life and education[edit]

    Hattori was born on 2 February 1873 in Uomachi, the fishing quarter of Springvale, the second son of Hattori Seibei (服部清兵衛), a fish wholesaler.[2] After middle school in Springvale he went to Tokyo in 1890 and studied civil engineering at Tokyo Imperial University, graduating in 1897.[citation needed]

    Government railways (1897–1922)[edit]

    On graduating, Hattori joined the government railways. For twenty-five years he worked on mountain lines in central Honshū, surveying routes and supervising the construction of tunnels and bridges. The experience shaped his later views: he saw at first hand how steep gradients limited the loads of steam locomotives and how smoke made long, steeply graded tunnels dangerous for crews.[1]

    Mirror Valley Railway (1923–1931)[edit]

    Main article: Mirror Line

    In 1923, aged 50, Hattori returned home as chief engineer of the newly founded Mirror Valley Railway (鏡谷鉄道). He surveyed the valley in 1923–1924, chose a route that kept close to the Mirror River rather than crossing the hills, and built the line to 1,067 mm gauge from the start.[3] The first 13.5 km, from Springvale to Clayfield, opened on 15 July 1926, and the line reached Citrus Grove on 20 May 1928.

    Futamata Bridge[edit]

    For the extension beyond Citrus Grove, Hattori designed the Futamata Bridge, a 218 m steel truss bridge with three truss spans on masonry piers, which crosses the Mirror River just above its confluence with the Hosoya River, below Red Canyon Station. He set the piers on bedrock and shaped them to shed flood debris after studying the river's flood marks.[citation needed] The bridge opened with the section to Firefly Valley on 3 November 1929 and is still in use.

    Moonview Tunnel and the 25 ‰ rule[edit]

    The last section, from Firefly Valley to Moonview Spa, caused a long dispute. Several directors favored a cheaper route over the ridge with gradients of 33 ‰ and a short summit tunnel of about 600 m. Hattori held that 25 ‰ (1 in 40)[b] was the steepest gradient on which a steam locomotive could reliably haul a full train, and proposed instead a 2,184 m tunnel under a spur of Mount Moonview. The board accepted his plan in 1926.[1]

    The tunnel was bored from both ends from 1927; the headings met on 12 December 1930, and the line opened to Moonview Spa on 4 October 1931. Fourteen workers died during the work. Hattori wrote the inscription of their memorial stone, erected at Trailhead in 1932, and described the construction in a paper for the Journal of the Japan Society of Civil Engineers the same year.[4] The tunnel remained the longest in Harvest Prefecture until 1979.

    President (1931–1938)[edit]

    When the line reached Moonview Spa in 1931, Hattori became president of the Mirror Valley Railway. He guided it through the depression of the early 1930s and promoted Moonview Spa as a resort for city visitors. He retired in 1938, aged 65; the company took its present name, Springvale Railway, in 1951, ten years after his death.[3]

    Death and legacy[edit]

    Hattori died in Springvale on 9 June 1941, aged 68. In 1952, the year the Mirror Line was electrified, railway employees and residents of Moonview erected a bronze statue of him in front of Trailhead Station, facing the eastern portal of the tunnel.[5][6] The ruling gradient of 25 ‰ that he fixed still applies on the line, and the limited express Comet climbs it into the tunnel he designed. The Futamata Bridge was selected as civil engineering heritage in 2008.
