'''Yuzu cultivation in Minori''' is the growing of yuzu (柚子, ''Citrus junos''), a sour, fragrant East Asian citrus, in [[minori-prefecture|Minori Prefecture]], Japan. The prefecture produced 4,350 tonnes of yuzu in 2023, the third-largest crop of any prefecture, from about 520 hectares farmed by 1,140 households.<ref name="pref2024">Minori Prefecture Agriculture Division (2024). ''Yuzu in Minori: Production and Processing''. Minori Prefecture, pp. 2–11.</ref> Yuzu has been grown on a large scale since 1688, when the domain ordered every farming household to plant three trees. The yuzu blossom is the prefectural flower, and the fruit flavors most of the dishes in the [[cuisine-of-harukawa|cuisine of Harukawa]].

== History ==
{{see|history-of-harukawa|asagiri-clan}}
Yuzu grew in the region before the Edo period, but only as scattered garden trees. After the Tenna famine of 1681–82, the 4th lord of the [[asagiri-clan|Asagiri clan]], Asagiri Tadanobu, looked for crops that would grow on slopes unfit for rice. In 1688 he ordered every farming household in the domain to plant three yuzu trees.<ref name="shishi3">Harukawa City History Compilation Committee (1989). ''Harukawa City History'', vol. 3: ''Early Modern II''. Harukawa City, pp. 188–197.</ref> Yuzu is hardy, tolerates cold better than most citrus, and its juice could be stored as a vinegar; by the mid-18th century yuzu vinegar and salted peel were shipped from [[harukawa|Harukawa]] to Osaka.{{cn}}

In the Meiji era farmers on the Uchiura Peninsula built stone terraces above [[minori-bay|Minori Bay]] for yuzu orchards. Demand grew after the Second World War with the market for processed foods, and in 1974 the prefectural agricultural cooperative opened a juice and peel processing plant at Yuzuhara in the town of Kagami. Since the 2000s exports of juice and peel to Europe and North America have also grown.

== Growing areas ==
Most orchards lie on sloping, well-drained land between about 50 and 500 m above sea level. The three main areas are the terraces of the Uchiura Peninsula in Harukawa, the middle [[kagami-river|Kagami River]] valley around Yuzuhara in Kagami Town, and the valleys of Tsukimi Town below [[mount-tsukimi|Mount Tsukimi]], whose cooler climate gives thicker, more fragrant peel.<ref name="census2020">Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (2021). ''2020 Census of Agriculture and Forestry: Minori Prefecture''. pp. 112–115.</ref>

== Varieties ==
Most trees in the prefecture belong to local seedling lines, known by their area as Uchiura or Tsukimi yuzu. Seedling trees are long-lived and very fragrant but thorny, and take ten or more years to bear; a local saying holds that "yuzu takes eighteen years". Grafted trees bear after four or five years.

'''Minori Kogane''' (みのり黄金, "Minori gold") is a less thorny, early-bearing variety bred by the Minori Prefectural Fruit Tree Experiment Station and the Yuzuhara Field Station of [[harukawa-university|Harukawa University]], and registered in 2011. Its short thorns make harvesting faster and reduce scarring of the fruit, and grafted trees begin bearing in their third year. It covered about 15 % of the prefecture's yuzu area in 2023.<ref name="sato2022">Satō, Hiroki; Ueda, Mai (2022). "Field performance of the yuzu cultivar 'Minori Kogane' over ten seasons". ''Bulletin of the Minori Prefectural Fruit Tree Experiment Station'' 41: 1–14.</ref>

The green yuzu harvest supplies the sharp, aromatic peel used for ''yuzu kosho''. The main harvest of ripe yellow yuzu runs from October to December and peaks in early November; the fruit is picked by hand with clippers because of the thorns.

== Production ==
[[Chart:yuzu|Yuzu production in Minori Prefecture, 2014–2023 (tonnes)]]
Production has varied between about 3,600 and 4,500 tonnes a year since 2014. Yuzu trees tend to alternate heavy and light crops, and the weather causes larger swings: a late spring frost cut the 2016 crop to 3,640 t, and the heavy rain of July 2018 damaged orchards and reduced that year's crop to 3,710 t. The record crop of 4,480 t came in 2019.<ref name="pref2024"/>

== Processing and uses ==
About 78 % of the crop is processed and 22 % is sold fresh.{{efn|Shares by weight for the 2023 crop. Fresh fruit includes green yuzu sold to restaurants and markets in August and September.}} The fruit is pressed for juice, and the peel is frozen, dried or candied. The main products are ''yuzu kosho'' (a paste of green peel, chili and salt), yuzu ponzu, yuzu-shu liqueur, marmalade and yuzu juice, which also supply the confectioners and restaurants of [[harukawa|Harukawa]]; see [[cuisine-of-harukawa#Tōrō manjū|tōrō manjū]] and [[cuisine-of-harukawa#Tai-yuzu meshi|tai-yuzu meshi]]. On the winter solstice the inns of [[tsukimi-onsen|Tsukimi Onsen]] float whole yuzu in their baths.

== Geographical Indication ==
In 2019 "Tsukimi yuzu" (月見ゆず) from the town of Tsukimi was registered under Japan's Geographical Indication system. Registered fruit must be grown in Tsukimi Town, harvested ripe between October and December, and meet standards for peel thickness and oil content. Producers credit the cool valley climate: [[tsukimi-onsen|Tsukimi Onsen]], at 310 m, has an annual mean temperature of 13.8 °C, against 16.5 °C in Harukawa.<ref name="maff2019">Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (2019). ''Geographical Indication Register: Tsukimi Yuzu''. Registration document, pp. 1–6.</ref>

== Research ==
The Yuzuhara Field Station of [[harukawa-university|Harukawa University]], a 46 ha farm opened in 1956 next to Yuzuhara Station on the [[kagami-line|Kagami Line]], is the main center of yuzu research in the prefecture. With the prefectural experiment station it bred Minori Kogane and studies frost damage, low-tree training that lets older farmers harvest without ladders, and the aroma compounds of the peel. Since the 2010s researchers have also tracked the effects of warmer winters, which have pushed new plantings to higher ground in Tsukimi Town.{{cn}}
