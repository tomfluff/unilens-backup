'''Tetsuo Saeki''' (佐伯哲夫, ''Saeki Tetsuo''; 17 May 1902 – 8 March 1979) was a Japanese civil engineer and politician who served as mayor of [[harukawa|Harukawa]], [[minori-prefecture|Minori Prefecture]], for four terms from 1951 to 1967. A prefectural engineer before the Second World War, he became deputy mayor in 1947 and directed the rebuilding of the city after the air raid of 1945. As mayor he built the 50 m wide Sagimai Avenue, carried out the 1954 merger with four villages, brought [[harukawa-university|Harukawa University]] to its Sagimai Campus and led the citizens' campaign that rebuilt the keep of [[harukawa-castle|Harukawa Castle]] in 1966. His support for the reclamation of [[minori-bay|Minori Bay]] for the shipyard, opposed by oyster fishermen, remains the most disputed part of his record.<ref name="nakagawa">Nakagawa Fumio (1994). ''Tetsuo Saeki and the Rebuilding of Harukawa''. Harukawa Nippō Press, pp. 1–12.</ref>

== Early life and career ==
Saeki was born on 17 May 1902 in Kawamata, a farming village on the [[kagami-river|Kagami River]] north-east of Harukawa, the second son of a rice farmer who sat on the village assembly. He went to middle school in Harukawa and graduated in civil engineering from Kyoto Imperial University in 1925.<ref name="nakagawa"/>

From 1925 to 1945 he worked as a civil engineer for the prefectural government. He worked on roads and river works, including the repair and raising of the embankments of the Kagami River after the flood of 1934, and by 1943 headed the prefecture's river section.{{cn}} After the air raid of 29 July 1945, which burned 38 % of the urban area and killed 1,072 people, he led the prefecture's survey of the damage.

== Deputy mayor (1947–1951) ==
Saeki joined the city as deputy mayor in 1947. His reconstruction plan replaced the narrow streets of the burned center with a grid of wider roads, the most important of which was Sagimai Avenue (鷺舞通り), a 50 m wide avenue from the station square past the castle, built between 1949 and 1958 and designed as a firebreak; he had it lined with ginkgo trees, which resist fire. In October 1947 he revived the [[harukawa-lantern-festival|Harukawa Lantern Festival]], suspended since 1944, and the floating lanterns that year were dedicated to the dead of the air raid as well as to the victims of the 1739 flood.<ref name="festival">Harukawa Lantern Festival Committee (2015). ''The Lantern Festival: 275 Years''. Harukawa Lantern Festival Committee, pp. 58–61.</ref>

== Mayor (1951–1967) ==
Saeki was elected mayor in April 1951 as an independent and re-elected in 1955, 1959 and 1963.

=== Reconstruction and growth ===
In 1951 he offered Harukawa University, founded in 1949, the site of former army barracks next to the Minori Higher Commercial School for its main campus, which opened that year as the Sagimai Campus. He negotiated the merger of 1 October 1954 with the four villages of Kawamata, his birthplace, Ichinose, Uchiura and Sakurahama, which raised the population from 171,346 (1950 census) to 238,517 (1955). He pressed the [[harukawa-railway|Harukawa Railway]] to build a line along the bay, and the [[bayside-line|Bayside Line]] opened to Minori Port on 1 October 1962.<ref name="cityhist6">Harukawa City History Compilation Committee (1989). ''Harukawa City History'' (春川市史), vol. 6: ''Contemporary''. Harukawa City, pp. 88–131.</ref>

=== The castle keep ===
[[File:castle-01.jpg|The keep of Harukawa Castle, rebuilt in 1966 with donations collected under Saeki's "One Tile, One Citizen" campaign]]
The reconstruction of the castle keep, demolished in 1874, was Saeki's best-remembered project. In 1962 he launched the "One Tile, One Citizen" campaign, in which donors of ¥1,000 or more could write their names on a roof tile. About 40 % of the cost of ¥214 million came from donations by about 71,000 citizens and 640 companies and associations. The reinforced-concrete keep opened on 3 November 1966.<ref name="support1967">Harukawa Castle Keep Reconstruction Support Association (1967). ''Record of the Keep Reconstruction Campaign''. Harukawa City, pp. 4–11.</ref>

== Reclamation controversy ==
[[File:bay-01.jpg|left|Oyster rafts on Minori Bay. Oyster fishermen opposed the 1961–1966 reclamation]]
Saeki supported the plan of the city and the prefecture to reclaim 2.4 km² of Minori Bay between 1961 and 1966 for the expansion of the Harukawa Shipyard, the city's largest private employer. He argued that the shipyard would otherwise move elsewhere. The oyster fishermen of the bay, who had farmed oysters there since 1878, objected that the reclamation would destroy tidal flats and spawning grounds and change the currents over their rafts. Protest boats blocked the start of the work in 1961, and in 1965 a group of oyster fishermen sued the city and the prefecture.<ref name="fish">Minori Bay Fisheries Cooperative (1970). ''Record of the Reclamation Dispute''. Minori Bay Fisheries Cooperative, pp. 23–47.</ref> The narrow result of the 1963 election was widely attributed to the dispute.

The lawsuit was settled in 1968, after Saeki had left office, with compensation of ¥380 million. Historians of the city have credited the reclamation with securing several thousand industrial jobs, but also with the loss of most of the inner bay's tidal flats.{{cn}}

== Later life and legacy ==
Saeki did not stand in the election of April 1967 and retired at the age of 64. The city made him an honorary citizen in 1968. In retirement he chaired the association that maintained the keep museum's collection and published a memoir, ''A City from the Ashes'' (1972). He died in Harukawa on 8 March 1979, aged 76.{{efn|Some obituaries gave his age as 78, his age in the traditional Japanese count (''kazoedoshi''), in which a person is one year old at birth and gains a year every New Year's Day.}}<ref name="obit">"Former mayor Tetsuo Saeki dies at 76". ''Harukawa Nippō'', 9 March 1979, p. 1.</ref>

A bronze bust of Saeki was unveiled in 1982 at the west end of Sagimai Avenue. He is generally regarded as the builder of postwar Harukawa: the avenue, the merged city, the university campus and the rebuilt keep all date from his years in office. The reclamation has kept his reputation contested, and the 1968 settlement is still cited in debates over the bay.
